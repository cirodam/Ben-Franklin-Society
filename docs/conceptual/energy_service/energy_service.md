# The Ben Franklin Society
## Of [County Name]

### Foundational Document on the Energy Service
*Article XII of the Bylaws*

---

## Preamble

Energy sovereignty is inseparable from community sovereignty. A community that depends entirely on distant fuel suppliers, centralized grids, and supply chains it cannot influence is a community whose resilience is borrowed rather than built. The Energy Service exists to build genuine energy resilience for the Ben Franklin Society — not the most elegant or efficient energy system, but the most resilient one. The organizing principle is simple: energy must keep flowing when external systems fail. Every decision the Energy Service makes is measured against that standard.

---

## Article I — Nature and Mandate

**§1. Core Principle.** Resilience is the Energy Service's organizing principle. The Energy Service does not seek the cheapest energy, the most technologically sophisticated energy, or the most efficient energy in the abstract. It seeks energy that keeps flowing when external supply chains are disrupted, when the grid fails, when fuel prices become unreachable, and when the conditions that modern energy systems depend upon no longer hold. Resilience and conservation together define what the Energy Service builds.

**§2. Existing Infrastructure.** The Energy Service shall leverage existing energy infrastructure to the greatest degree possible. Members already possess solar panels, generators, storage batteries, wood stoves, propane systems, and other energy assets. The Energy Service maps, supports, connects, and builds upon what already exists rather than replacing it. New community infrastructure complements and extends what the community already has.

**§3. Distributed Generation.** The Energy Service shall prioritize distributed energy generation over centralized generation wherever feasible. Distributed systems — solar installations at household and community scale, local fuel storage, multiple generation sources — fail gracefully when components fail. Centralized systems fail catastrophically. Resilience favors distribution.

**§4. Energy Domains.** The Energy Service is responsible for four energy domains: liquid fuels for transportation and equipment; electricity generation and transmission; cooking fuel; and heating fuel. Together these domains constitute the community's complete energy needs. The Energy Service shall develop and maintain capacity across all four.

**§5. Conservation.** Conservation and efficiency are essential components of energy resilience. Energy that is not consumed does not need to be produced or stored. The Energy Service shall actively promote conservation and efficiency across all four energy domains — through member support, community infrastructure design, and such other means as the Engineering College determines effective.

**§6. Oversight.** The Energy Service is overseen by the Engineering College — a body of peers with competence in electrical, mechanical, and civil infrastructure systems. The Engineering College sets technical standards for the Energy Service, credentials energy practitioners, and maintains the technical knowledge base on which energy resilience depends.

## Article II — Electricity Generation and Transmission

**§7. Generation Mandate.** The Energy Service shall develop and maintain community electrical generation capacity sufficient to meet critical community needs independently of the external grid. Critical needs include the community's Services — health, food, financial infrastructure — and such member needs as the Assembly determines essential. Grid independence for critical functions is the baseline goal.

**§8. Solar Priority.** Solar photovoltaic generation shall be the primary community electricity generation technology. Solar is locally producible in the community's climate, requires no fuel supply chain, and can be deployed at household and community scale simultaneously. The Energy Service shall develop community solar capacity while supporting member solar installations as part of a unified distributed generation approach.

**§9. Storage.** Electrical storage — battery systems capable of sustaining critical functions through periods of low generation — is as important as generation capacity. The Energy Service shall develop community battery storage in parallel with generation capacity. LiFePO4 and similar chemistries suited to long cycle life and safety shall be prioritized. *[Placeholder: storage standards to be established by Engineering College.]*

**§10. Grid Relationship.** The Energy Service shall work with the external electrical grid where it is available and functional, treating it as a supplement to community generation rather than a dependency. Community generation and storage shall be sized and configured to operate independently when grid power is unavailable.

**§11. Transmission.** The Energy Service shall develop community electrical transmission infrastructure connecting generation sources, storage systems, and critical loads. Transmission infrastructure shall be designed for resilience — capable of operating in partial configurations when components fail. *[Placeholder: transmission standards and priority load definitions to be established by Engineering College with Assembly approval.]*

**§12. Member Electricity Support.** The Energy Service shall actively support members in developing their own electrical generation and storage capacity. Every household with solar panels and battery storage reduces the burden on community infrastructure and increases the community's total distributed generation capacity. Member support for electricity is both a service and a resilience investment.

## Article III — Liquid Fuels

**§13. Mandate.** Liquid fuels — gasoline, diesel, and such other liquid fuels as the community uses — power transportation, farm equipment, emergency generators, and other critical systems that electricity cannot yet replace. The Energy Service is responsible for liquid fuel supply, storage, and distribution to the community.

**§14. Supply Chain Awareness.** Liquid fuel supply chains are among the most geopolitically exposed energy systems the community depends upon. The Energy Service shall maintain current awareness of liquid fuel supply conditions and shall advise the Assembly when supply disruption risks are elevated. Liquid fuel resilience requires active monitoring, not passive assumption of availability.

**§15. Strategic Reserve.** The Energy Service shall maintain a strategic reserve of liquid fuels sufficient to sustain critical community operations through periods of supply disruption. Reserve categories shall include at minimum gasoline and diesel. *[Placeholder: reserve levels and storage standards to be established by Engineering College with Assembly approval.]*

**§16. Rotation.** Liquid fuel reserves shall be actively rotated to maintain fuel quality. The Energy Service shall establish rotation protocols ensuring that stored fuel remains within its useful life. A reserve of degraded fuel is not a reserve.

**§17. Reduction.** Long-term liquid fuel resilience requires reducing liquid fuel dependence. As electrical capacity grows and farm equipment electrification becomes feasible, the Energy Service shall actively work to reduce the community's liquid fuel requirements. Liquid fuel dependency is a strategic vulnerability. Reducing it is a long-term resilience goal.

## Article IV — Cooking Fuel

**§18. Mandate.** Cooking fuel is among the most basic energy needs of community life. A community that cannot cook its food has not solved food security regardless of how much food it stores. The Energy Service shall ensure that cooking fuel — propane, wood, biogas, or such other fuels as the community uses — is available to members and that the community's cooking infrastructure can function through external supply disruptions.

**§19. Fuel Diversity.** The Energy Service shall support diversity in cooking fuel sources. A community that can cook with propane, with wood, and with electricity is far more resilient than one dependent on a single cooking fuel. The Energy Service shall actively encourage and support cooking fuel diversity among members and in community kitchen infrastructure.

**§20. Wood and Biomass.** Wood and biomass cooking fuel represents the community's most locally producible and supply-chain-independent cooking energy source. The Energy Service shall support sustainable wood fuel production and use within the community, in coordination with the Agricultural Service on land management and the Engineering College on efficient combustion.

**§21. Reserve.** The Energy Service shall maintain a reserve of cooking fuel sufficient to sustain community kitchen operations and member cooking needs through periods of external supply disruption. Reserve categories shall reflect the community's primary cooking fuel sources. *[Placeholder: cooking fuel reserve levels to be established by Engineering College with Assembly approval.]*

**§22. Coordination with Food Service.** The Energy Service shall coordinate with the Food Service on cooking fuel needs for community kitchens. The Food Service's ability to fulfill its mandate depends directly on the Energy Service's cooking fuel function. The two services shall maintain active planning coordination.

## Article V — Heating Fuel

**§23. Mandate.** Heating fuel is a life-safety need for vulnerable members — the elderly, the disabled, infants, and the ill — during cold periods. While the community's climate is relatively mild, heating failure during cold weather is a genuine health and safety risk that the Energy Service takes seriously. The Energy Service shall ensure that heating fuel is available to members and that the community can sustain heating through supply disruptions.

**§24. Vulnerable Members.** The Energy Service shall coordinate with the Caregivers College and the Health Service to identify members most vulnerable to heating failure and to ensure that their heating needs are met as a priority. Heating failure for a vulnerable member is a health emergency. The Energy Service shall treat vulnerable member heating as a critical function.

**§25. Fuel Diversity.** The Energy Service shall support heating fuel diversity — propane, wood, passive solar design, and such other heating sources as are appropriate to the community's climate. Diversity of heating fuel sources reduces the impact of any single supply disruption on the community's ability to stay warm.

**§26. Weatherization.** Reducing heat loss through weatherization is among the most cost-effective heating resilience investments available. The Energy Service shall actively support member weatherization — insulation, draft sealing, window improvement — as part of its conservation mandate. A home that retains heat requires less fuel to stay warm and remains habitable longer when heating systems fail.

**§27. Reserve.** The Energy Service shall maintain a reserve of heating fuel sufficient to meet critical heating needs through periods of supply disruption, with priority to vulnerable members. *[Placeholder: heating fuel reserve levels to be established by Engineering College with Assembly approval.]*

## Article VI — Member Energy Support

**§28. Mandate.** The Energy Service shall actively support members in improving their own energy resilience. Every household that generates its own electricity, stores its own energy, heats efficiently, and reduces fuel dependence strengthens the community's collective resilience. Member energy support is both a service to individual members and a community resilience investment.

**§29. Assessment.** The Energy Service shall offer energy assessments to members — evaluating their current energy systems, identifying vulnerabilities, and recommending improvements. Assessments are offered as a service, not an inspection. The Energy Service serves members; it does not surveil them.

**§30. Installation Support.** The Energy Service shall support members in installing energy systems — solar panels, battery storage, wood stoves, insulation, and such other resilience improvements as members wish to make. Support may include technical advice, labor coordination, and access to community equipment and expertise.

**§31. Knowledge Transmission.** The Energy Service shall actively transmit energy knowledge within the community — how to install and maintain solar systems, how to size battery storage, how to operate wood heating systems safely and efficiently, how to weatherize effectively. Energy knowledge distributed broadly across the community is more resilient than energy knowledge concentrated in the Energy Service's staff.

**§32. Priority Support.** The Energy Service shall prioritize energy support for members who are most vulnerable to energy insecurity — the elderly, the disabled, and those with limited resources. Energy vulnerability is a health and safety risk. The Energy Service shall coordinate with the Caregivers College and the Social Insurance Fund to ensure vulnerable members receive appropriate energy support.

## Article VII — The Energy Reserve

**§33. Obligation.** The Energy Service shall maintain strategic reserves across all four energy domains. Energy reserves are a resilience obligation, not an optional enhancement. The reserve is what sustains the community when external energy supply fails.

**§34. Reserve Categories.** The energy reserve shall include at minimum: liquid fuel reserves of gasoline and diesel; cooking fuel reserves including propane and wood fuel; and heating fuel reserves appropriate to the community's primary heating sources. Electrical storage capacity built into the community's generation infrastructure constitutes the electrical energy reserve.

**§35. Reserve Standards.** Minimum reserve levels for each category shall be established by the Engineering College with Assembly approval and reviewed annually. Reserve levels shall be set with reference to the community's critical operational needs and realistic supply disruption scenarios. *[Placeholder: reserve minimums to be established by Engineering College with Assembly approval.]*

**§36. Rotation and Maintenance.** All energy reserves shall be actively maintained — rotated to preserve quality, inspected regularly, and stored safely. A degraded or unsafe reserve is not a reserve. The Energy Service shall maintain protocols for each reserve category appropriate to that fuel's storage requirements.

**§37. Reporting.** Reserve levels shall be reported to the Assembly quarterly. Any material decline below established minimums shall be reported immediately. Reserve status shall be included in the Energy Service's annual report.

## Article VIII — Inter-Society Energy Coordination

**§38. Network.** The Energy Service shall maintain active relationships with the energy services of other Ben Franklin Societies. Energy equipment, technical knowledge, and fuel reserves are resources that benefit from sharing across the federation network — particularly during acute supply disruptions when no single society can meet all its needs alone.

**§39. Technical Knowledge Exchange.** The Engineering Colleges of member societies shall maintain active knowledge exchange on energy systems — what technologies are performing well, what installation approaches are most effective in the region's climate, what failure modes are being encountered. Community-scale energy knowledge networks are more responsive and locally relevant than any centralized technical resource.

**§40. Emergency Coordination.** In energy emergencies — acute fuel shortages, generation failures, extreme weather events — the Energy Service shall coordinate with other societies to share reserves and technical capacity as available. Inter-society energy mutual aid is an expression of federation solidarity in one of its most practically important forms.

## Article IX — Transparency and Reporting

**§41. Annual Report.** The Energy Service shall publish an annual report covering: community generation capacity by source; storage capacity; reserve levels across all categories; member support engagements; weatherization and efficiency activity; conservation outcomes; inter-society coordination activity; and significant infrastructure developments. The report shall be presented to the Assembly and made available to all members.

**§42. Resilience Metric.** The annual report shall include a community energy resilience metric — an assessment of how long the community could sustain critical operations on its own generation and reserves without external energy supply. This metric shall be tracked over time as a measure of the Energy Service's progress toward its core purpose.

**§43. Reserve Reporting.** Reserve levels across all energy categories shall be reported to the Assembly quarterly. Any material decline below established minimums shall be reported immediately.
