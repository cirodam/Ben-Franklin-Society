# The Ben Franklin Society
## Charter
### A Definition Held in Common by All Member Societies

---

## Preamble

There are moments in history that test the character of communities — that demand more than ordinary times prepare anyone for. We believe we are living in one. We do not take that lightly. We resolve to meet it together.

Benjamin Franklin did not wait for someone else to build what his community needed. He built it with his own hands and his neighbors'. A fire company. A library. A hospital. A philosophical society. He understood that a community's resilience lives in its institutions — and that those institutions have to be built before the crisis, not during it.

We found Ben Franklin Societies in that spirit. Not as a political movement, not as an ideology, not as a statement of what we are against. As a commitment to what we are building. Wherever a Ben Franklin Society exists, it exists because a community decided to stop waiting and start building.

This Charter defines what a Ben Franklin Society is. It is held in common by all member societies. No single society may change it. It is our shared definition of ourselves.

---

## Part One — Founding Principles

These are the principles every Ben Franklin Society holds. They are not a political platform. They are a description of what we are. A society that abandons these principles has ceased to be a Ben Franklin Society in any meaningful sense.

**I. Human Dignity**
We hold that human dignity is unconditional. It cannot be earned or lost, granted or revoked. You have it because you are human — wherever you were born, whatever you have done, whoever you are. This is the foundation everything else rests on.

**II. Self-Governance**
We govern ourselves through genuine deliberation. No authority is legitimate here that does not flow from the genuine consent of the people subject to it. We trust ordinary people to make good decisions — not ideology, not party, not wealth.

**III. Honest Inquiry**
We believe in honest inquiry. The world is what it is regardless of what we wish it to be. We commit to following evidence wherever it leads.

**IV. Mutual Obligation**
We accept collective responsibility for every member's food, care, and security. Mutual obligation, not charity.

**V. Economic Sovereignty**
Economic sovereignty belongs to the people who live and work in a community. We reject systematic exploitation in all its forms.

**VI. Building Our Own**
We build our own institutions rather than waiting for failing ones. We are practitioners first — we measure success not by what we believe but by what our community can do.

**VII. Stewardship**
We are stewards of the natural world. We treat land, water, and living systems as held in trust for those who come after us.

**VIII. Common Purpose**
We protect our capacity to think and decide for ourselves. We build community life that restores genuine connection and common purpose.

**IX. Accountability**
We hold ourselves to genuine accountability — to each other, to our principles, and to all people.

**X. Human Decency**
We hold ourselves to basic human decency. How we treat each other is not separate from what we are building — it is what we are building.

**XI. Service**
We hold that positions of leadership and governance exist solely to serve the people subject to them. Leadership is a burden accepted, not a privilege claimed. Anyone who seeks governance authority for personal gain, status, or power is unfit to hold it.

XII. The Limits of Markets
We acknowledge that markets are ill-suited to providing certain categories of goods. In particular those where human need is too urgent to permit the discipline of price, those where the profit motive is directly opposed to the purpose of the service, and those where the full cost of provision or the full value of preservation cannot be captured in a transaction between buyer and seller.

XIII. Liberty
The liberty of all people is sacred. The Society explicitly rejects incarceration as a response to poverty, addiction, mental illness, or social conditions the Society itself is obligated to address.

## Part Two — What Every Ben Franklin Society Must Have

A Ben Franklin Society is not merely a community that holds the founding principles. It is a community that has built the institutions those principles require. The following are the institutional minimums — what a community must have and maintain to call itself a Ben Franklin Society. These are not aspirations. They are requirements.

**1. Community Authority**
All authority originates with and belongs to the community in its entirety. A Ben Franklin Society must maintain this principle in its institutional design — not as a statement of belief but as an operational reality. Authority is delegated downward for practical purposes and is always recallable upward. The community has final say over all institutions, bodies, and decisions within the Society.

**2. General Assembly**
A Ben Franklin Society must maintain a General Assembly drawn by sortition from the eligible adult membership. The General Assembly is the primary legislative body of the Society, acting by delegation from the community. It must be genuinely sortition-drawn — no elections, no appointments, no hereditary positions. The randomness of selection is not a procedural detail. It is the mechanism by which the General Assembly remains the community's body rather than any faction's instrument.

**3. The Frank**
A Ben Franklin Society must issue and maintain the Frank as its community currency. The Frank is issued on the basis of the person-year — equal for every member — and flows through the Treasury into the community's common life. The Frank is the material expression of the community's economic sovereignty. A Ben Franklin Society that does not control its own currency has not achieved the economic sovereignty Principle V requires.

**4. Financial Infrastructure**
A Ben Franklin Society must maintain the foundational financial institutions without which a Frank economy cannot function: a Central Bank responsible for Frank issuance and the membership roll; a Community Bank maintaining the ledger of Frank holdings and transactions; a Treasury receiving dues and disbursing funds per General Assembly appropriation; and a Social Insurance Fund providing regular support to elderly and disabled members. These four institutions are not optional. They are the infrastructure of economic self-governance.

**5. The Colleges**
A Ben Franklin Society must maintain the Colleges framework — the system of peer bodies through which the community acknowledges domain expertise and draws qualified practitioners for governance in specialized domains. The specific Colleges a society maintains will vary with its size, population, and development. The framework itself — peer credentialing, qualified sortition, General Assembly creation and accountability — must be maintained. A Ben Franklin Society governs itself through qualified democracy, not through either unqualified populism or expert technocracy.

**6. Food Security**
A Ben Franklin Society must treat food security as a community obligation. No member shall go without adequate nutrition through failure of the community to act. The Society must maintain the institutional capacity to ensure this — through food services, food reserves, and agricultural capacity development. The specific institutions may vary. The obligation does not.

**7. Healthcare**
A Ben Franklin Society must treat healthcare as a right of membership. The Society must maintain health services at whatever level of capacity it can sustain, working continuously to expand that capacity. No member shall be without access to the best healthcare the community can provide. The Society plans explicitly for circumstances in which conventional medical resources are scarce or absent — resilience is built in, not assumed.

**8. Shelter**
A Ben Franklin Society must guarantee that no member is without adequate shelter through failure of the community to act. The Society must maintain the institutional capacity to fulfill this guarantee — through housing services, emergency shelter, and maintenance support. Shelter is a human necessity. Its guarantee is a charter requirement.

**9. Human Dignity in Practice**
A Ben Franklin Society must maintain institutional protections for human dignity that give Principle I operational force. No member shall be removed from membership, deprived of their rights, or subjected to institutional action without due process. No institution of the Society shall treat any member as less than fully human. The unconditional dignity of Principle I is not merely a statement of belief — it is a constitutional constraint on every institution the Society maintains.

**10. Fair Dealing with Neighboring Societies**
A Ben Franklin Society must deal fairly with other member societies. The economic sovereignty guaranteed to members within a society extends, in principle, to the relations between societies. A Ben Franklin Society may not use control of essential resources — water, land access, transport routes, food supply, or infrastructure — to coerce, extract from, or systematically disadvantage another society. Trade and mutual aid between societies must be conducted in good faith. A society that leverages necessity into exploitation has violated the same principle it claims to uphold for its own members. Complaints of inter-society exploitation are subject to Federation mediation and may constitute grounds for compliance review.

**11. Annual Review of Founding Purpose**
A Ben Franklin Society must convene an annual meeting dedicated to honest examination of whether the Society remains true to its founding purpose and principles. This is not a celebratory formality. It is a deliberate moment of institutional self-scrutiny. The meeting must engage these questions directly: Are we still serving the purpose we were founded to serve? Have our founding assumptions proven sound or do they require revision? Are our institutions achieving what they were designed to achieve? Where have we failed, and what must change? This annual review is not optional. It is the mechanism by which the Society maintains the honest inquiry required by Principle III and prevents institutional drift from charter obligations. The review findings must be made available to all members and must inform the General Assembly's legislative priorities for the following year.

## Part Three — The Nature of This Charter

**Held in Common**
This Charter is held collectively by all Ben Franklin Societies. It is not the property of any single society, any founding body, or any individual. It belongs to the network of communities that have adopted it and committed to living by it. No single society may amend it. Changes to the Charter require the deliberate consensus of the societies that hold it.

**A Definition, Not a Constitution**
This Charter defines what a Ben Franklin Society is. It does not govern how any particular society governs itself. Each society maintains its own constitution — its own foundational document establishing the specific institutions, processes, and commitments by which it lives the Charter's principles and maintains the Charter's institutional minimums. A society's constitution may go further than this Charter requires. It may not fall short of it.

**A Living Standard**
The Charter is a living standard — a definition that communities are measured against, not just at founding but continuously. A Ben Franklin Society that abandons its General Assembly, that surrenders its Frank, that allows its members to go without food or shelter or care, has ceased to be a Ben Franklin Society in any meaningful sense. The Charter names what we are. Living up to it is the ongoing work.

**Building Together**
Benjamin Franklin built what his community needed. We build what ours needs. Every Ben Franklin Society, wherever it exists, is part of that same project — the ancient human project of neighbors deciding to take care of each other. We do not take that lightly. We resolve to meet it together.
