# Vision and Philosophical Foundation

## Core Vision

A Ben Franklin Society is a group of people who work together to meet each other's basic needs for food, housing, and healthcare. It's a tool for cooperation.

The expectation is that existing institutions people rely on will likely become less reliable and more dysfunctional with time. A Ben Franklin Society takes nothing for granted. It's built to function independently of those systems if needed.

## On Institutional Decline

The United States is likely in decline. There are probably many reasons—it may be that the existing system is too complex and must fall to a lower level of complexity, or it could be any number of smaller factors.

The timeline is uncertain—probably years or decades. It will happen at different speeds in different areas. Eventually it stops when society finds a level of complexity it can sustain.

From the perspective of the average American, this will look like institutions becoming increasingly unreliable and dysfunctional. They may disappear altogether.

There is much we cannot know for certain. But this general trend seems right, at least for now. The answer is local resilience, redundancy, flexibility, and cooperation.

At the very least, we can't rely on traditional institutions at this point.

## Philosophical Principles

### On Human Capability

If you give average Americans structured deliberation and time, they can essentially always reach thoughtful, nuanced, well-reasoned decisions.

### On Political Division

Americans have far more in common than they have as differences. The false binary of left vs. right frequently forces people to defend positions that belong to their group, not that they reasoned their way to.

Most people are probably in near agreement on most issues. People who disagree with a group's decision will generally accept it if they trust the process.

### On Local Context

Decisions that genuinely make sense for one area may not make sense for another. What works in dense urban environments may not work in rural areas. Local governance allows for local solutions.

### On Judgment Over Rules

Trust human judgment. We can't afford to codify every single little thing. But we can help the average person ask the questions needed to make the best possible decision. It's through that process—through practice—that we cultivate better judgment.

### On Technology

Software is a tool, not the institution itself. The real work happens between people. Technology should serve human flourishing, not extract value or manipulate behavior.

### On Governance and Sortition

Sortition—random selection of citizens for governance roles—is an unusually good answer to the challenges we face today. It eliminates or weakens career politicians, gerrymandering, and money in politics. When ordinary citizens govern for a term and then return to regular life, that's true self-governance. You can't buy influence over people you can't predict. You can't build a political career when selection is random. Power doesn't accumulate.

### On Coordination and Economic Independence

Existing economic structures require money as the intermediary for coordination. When money is scarce, coordination breaks down entirely—even when willing workers and real needs both exist. A Ben Franklin Society provides its own coordination infrastructure through an internal accounting system that enables exchange without dependence on external monetary scarcity. This system is internal to the community, generated through contribution rather than extracted from outside, and designed for coordination and mutual support rather than accumulation or extraction.

### On Accountability and Consequences

People treat decisions very seriously when they can see the consequences of their decisions. It's one thing to vote for a politician with an ideological position. It's another thing to make that decision yourself, face-to-face with the person it affects. When you govern people you know and will live with, when you'll experience the consequences of what you decide, you make more humane and thoughtful choices. This is why local governance by ordinary citizens produces better outcomes than distant political abstractions.

### On the Decline of Social Capital

Americans used to participate in churches, unions, fraternal lodges, bowling leagues, PTAs. These weren't just leisure—they were the fabric of social capital. Trust, reciprocity, mutual obligation, shared purpose. That fabric has unraveled. People are isolated. When crisis hits, they have no community to fall back on. A Ben Franklin Society holds that this decline is real and directly attempts to fight it—rebuilding what has been lost, not through nostalgia, but through practical necessity.

### On Social Media and Extractive Technology

Social media platforms are designed to extract value through manipulation. Engagement metrics, outrage amplification, addiction mechanics. The business model requires keeping you scrolling, not helping you flourish. This is technology as exploitation.

Social media rewards outrage, punishes nuance, and amplifies tribal signaling. It makes genuine deliberation nearly impossible. The worst versions of people's opinions get the most visibility.

A BFS rejects this entirely. Technology should serve human needs, not corporate extraction. The platform creates space for structured deliberation—where people have time, context, and incentive to think carefully instead of performing for an audience.

### On Human Dignity

Human dignity is equal, sacred, and inviolable. Every member has inherent worth that doesn't depend on productivity, wealth, status, or ability. This isn't charity—it's recognition of fundamental human value. A society that allows members to go without food, housing, or healthcare when the community has capacity to provide them has failed its most basic obligation.

### On Contribution and Reciprocity

A person should work if work is required for the community's subsistence and they're able. This is mutual obligation—everyone contributes what they can to support each other. But if someone is unable to work, or there is no work to be found, that isn't their fault. The community's obligation to support members doesn't depend on their productivity. Dignity and contribution are both important, but dignity comes first.
