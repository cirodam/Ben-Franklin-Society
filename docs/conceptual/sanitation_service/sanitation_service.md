# The Ben Franklin Society
## Of [County Name]

### Foundational Document on the Sanitation Service
*Article XXIV of the Bylaws*

---

## Preamble

Sanitation is among the most consequential public health achievements in human history. The management of human waste and solid refuse — boring, unglamorous, and entirely essential — has done more to extend human life and reduce preventable disease than almost any other community function. When sanitation works, nobody notices. When it fails, disease follows with devastating speed. The Sanitation Service exists to ensure that the community's waste streams are managed safely, reliably, and — wherever possible — productively. Organic waste becomes compost. Metals become recovered materials. Hazardous waste is handled safely. What cannot be recovered is disposed of without threatening community health. The Service works with whatever infrastructure exists and builds from there.

---

## Article I — Nature and Mandate

**§1. Public Health Foundation.** The Sanitation Service's foundational mandate is public health protection. The safe management of human waste and solid refuse is a non-negotiable community obligation. No consideration — resource constraint, operational difficulty, or competing priority — justifies compromising the public health foundation. Everything else the Service does — composting, recycling, material recovery — is built on and around that foundation, never at its expense.

**§2. Working with What Exists.** The Sanitation Service works with the waste management infrastructure that exists within the community — septic systems, composting toilets, municipal sewage connections, existing collection vehicles, transfer stations, and landfill access. It does not assume dedicated new infrastructure. It manages existing systems reliably, improves them where possible, and develops community capacity from what is available.

**§3. Material Flows.** The Sanitation Service approaches waste streams as material flows — sources of recoverable value as well as material requiring disposal. Organic waste is compost feedstock. Metal is recoverable material. The Service shall maximize material recovery from waste streams consistent with safe and reliable waste management.

**§4. Functions.** The Sanitation Service carries out its mandate through the following functions: human waste management; solid waste collection and disposal; organic waste transfer for composting; metal and material recovery; hazardous waste coordination; and public health monitoring.

**§5. Oversight.** The Sanitation Service is overseen by the Engineering College, which sets technical standards for waste management systems and operations. The Medical College advises on public health standards, monitoring requirements, and the health implications of sanitation conditions. Safe sanitation is both an engineering and a medical question — both Colleges shall participate actively in oversight.

## Article II — Human Waste Management

**§6. Mandate.** The Sanitation Service is responsible for ensuring that human waste is managed safely throughout the community. Unsafe human waste management is among the most immediate public health threats a community can face — it contaminates water sources, spreads disease, and makes inhabited areas genuinely dangerous. No member shall lack access to safe human waste management.

**§7. Existing Systems.** The community's human waste management relies on a mix of septic systems, composting toilets, pit latrines, and connections to municipal sewage infrastructure where available. The Sanitation Service shall assess, support, and maintain all of these systems — working with what exists rather than assuming uniform infrastructure.

**§8. System Assessment.** The Sanitation Service shall assess the condition and adequacy of human waste management systems throughout the community — identifying failing septic systems, inadequate facilities, and members without safe waste management. Assessment is the basis for prioritized maintenance and improvement.

**§9. Maintenance Support.** The Sanitation Service shall provide maintenance support for human waste systems — septic pumping, system inspection, repair coordination, and such other maintenance as keeps systems functioning safely. Priority shall be given to systems that are failing or at risk of failure, and to members who cannot maintain their systems independently.

**§10. Water Source Protection.** Human waste management and water source protection are directly connected — failing septic systems and inadequate waste management contaminate wells and water sources. The Sanitation Service shall coordinate with the Water Service on the relationship between waste management systems and water sources throughout the community. Contamination prevention is the shared goal.

**§11. Composting Toilets and Alternative Systems.** Where composting toilets and other alternative human waste management systems are in use or appropriate the Sanitation Service shall support their proper operation and shall coordinate the transfer of processed humanure to the Agricultural Service for composting where conditions allow. Alternative systems that safely close the nutrient loop contribute to the community's agricultural resilience.

## Article III — Solid Waste Collection and Disposal

**§12. Collection.** The Sanitation Service shall organize reliable solid waste collection throughout the community. Collection schedules shall reflect available resources — vehicles, fuel, personnel — and shall be published and maintained reliably. A collection service that fails to collect creates immediate public health problems. The Service shall run what it commits to running.

**§13. Disposal.** Waste that cannot be recovered, composted, or recycled shall be disposed of safely — through landfill access, transfer station coordination, or such other disposal methods as are available and safe. The Sanitation Service shall maintain working relationships with external waste disposal infrastructure while building community capacity to reduce dependence on it.

**§14. Waste Reduction.** The Sanitation Service shall actively work to reduce the volume of waste requiring disposal — through composting, recycling, material recovery, and community education on waste reduction. Less waste generated means less waste to manage. The Service shall treat waste reduction as a standing operational priority alongside collection and disposal.

**§15. Community Education.** The Sanitation Service shall educate members on waste separation, composting, recycling, and waste reduction. Effective material recovery depends on members separating their waste correctly at the source. Education delivered through the Education Service and direct community outreach is an essential part of the Sanitation Service's function.

**§16. Coordination with Transit and Courier Services.** Collection vehicles running waste routes travel the same roads as transit and courier vehicles. The Sanitation, Transit, and Courier Services shall coordinate routes and schedules to maximize efficiency and minimize total vehicle fuel consumption.

## Article IV — Organic Waste and Composting

**§17. Organic Waste Mandate.** Organic waste — food scraps, agricultural residue, yard material, and appropriate human waste products — is not waste in the meaningful sense. It is compost feedstock, a soil amendment that feeds back into the community's agricultural capacity. The Sanitation Service shall separate, collect, and transfer organic waste to the Agricultural Service as a primary function, not a secondary one.

**§18. Agricultural Service Coordination.** The Sanitation Service and Agricultural Service shall maintain a close and formal working relationship on organic waste transfer. The Agricultural Service's composting capacity determines how much organic material the Sanitation Service can transfer. The Sanitation Service's collection capacity determines what organic material reaches the Agricultural Service. Both services shall plan together.

**§19. Food Service Coordination.** The Food Service generates significant organic waste through its kitchen operations. The Sanitation Service shall coordinate with the Food Service on organic waste collection and transfer — ensuring that kitchen food waste flows efficiently into the composting system rather than into general waste disposal.

**§20. Member Composting.** The Sanitation Service shall support members in composting their own organic waste at home — providing guidance, resources, and such technical support as helps members close the nutrient loop at the household level. Distributed household composting reduces the volume of organic material requiring collection and transport.

**§21. Safety.** Organic waste management must be conducted safely — improperly managed organic material creates odor, attracts pests, and can harbor pathogens. The Sanitation Service shall maintain organic waste handling standards established by the Engineering College and Medical College that ensure composting operations are safe for workers and the community.

## Article V — Metal and Material Recovery

**§22. Recovery Mandate.** The Sanitation Service shall actively recover materials from waste streams that have value to the community — primarily metals, but also glass, certain plastics, and such other recoverable materials as the community can usefully process or trade. Material recovery from waste is both economically valuable and waste-volume-reducing.

**§23. Metal Recovery.** Metal recovered from waste streams is among the most practically valuable materials the Sanitation Service can recover. Scrap metal has direct utility for the community's fabrication and maintenance capacity — the Engineering College's repair functions, member fabricators, and inter-society trade. The Sanitation Service shall establish metal collection, sorting, and transfer protocols that make recovered metal available to the community's productive capacity.

**§24. Engineering College Coordination.** The Sanitation Service and Engineering College shall coordinate on material recovery — what materials are most useful to the community, how they should be sorted and stored, and how they are made available for repair, fabrication, and other productive uses. Recovered materials are community resources; their distribution shall serve community needs.

**§25. Commerce Service Integration.** Recovered materials that exceed community productive needs may be traded through the Commerce Service marketplace. Surplus scrap metal, glass, and other recoverable materials have Frank economy value. The Sanitation Service shall coordinate with the Commerce Service on making recovered materials available for community trade.

**§26. Member Separation.** Effective material recovery requires members to separate recyclable materials from general waste at the source. The Sanitation Service shall establish clear and simple separation guidelines, provide appropriate containers, and educate members on what to separate and why. Separation that members actually do consistently is more valuable than elaborate systems that members don't follow.

## Article VI — Hazardous Waste

**§27. Mandate.** Hazardous waste — chemicals, batteries, electronics, agricultural chemicals, medical waste, and such other materials as pose particular risks to human health and the environment — requires handling distinct from general waste. The Sanitation Service is responsible for coordinating safe hazardous waste management within the community.

**§28. Fire and Emergency Service Coordination.** The Fire and Emergency Service maintains hazardous materials response expertise that the Sanitation Service depends upon for safe hazardous waste management. The two services shall maintain active coordination protocols — the Fire and Emergency Service advising on safe handling and responding to hazardous waste incidents, the Sanitation Service managing ongoing collection and disposal. Neither service manages hazardous waste in isolation from the other.

**§29. Collection Events.** The Sanitation Service shall organize regular hazardous waste collection events where members can safely dispose of household hazardous materials — paints, solvents, batteries, electronics, and such other materials as should not enter the general waste stream. Hazardous waste that members have no safe disposal option for ends up in the environment or in the general waste stream. Collection events prevent this.

**§30. Agricultural Chemical Waste.** Agricultural communities generate significant chemical waste — expired pesticides, fertilizer residue, fuel containers, and such other agricultural chemical materials as require careful disposal. The Sanitation Service shall develop specific protocols for agricultural chemical waste in coordination with the Agricultural Service and the Fire and Emergency Service.

**§31. Medical Waste.** Medical waste from the Health Service and from member households requires specific handling. The Sanitation Service and Health Service shall establish protocols for safe medical waste collection and disposal that protect both sanitation workers and the broader community.

**§32. External Coordination.** Hazardous waste disposal often requires external facilities and regulatory coordination. The Sanitation Service shall maintain working relationships with external hazardous waste disposal infrastructure and shall comply with applicable legal requirements while building community capacity to handle hazardous materials safely.

## Article VII — Public Health Monitoring

**§33. Monitoring Mandate.** The Sanitation Service shall monitor sanitation conditions throughout the community — assessing waste management system adequacy, identifying emerging public health risks from waste, and reporting to the Medical College and Health Service on sanitation-related health concerns. Sanitation failure announces itself through disease if it is not caught through monitoring first.

**§34. Medical College Coordination.** The Medical College shall establish public health monitoring standards for sanitation and shall advise the Sanitation Service on the health implications of observed conditions. The Sanitation Service provides the operational data; the Medical College provides the public health interpretation. Together they form the community's sanitation public health function.

**§35. Water Service Coordination.** Sanitation monitoring and water quality monitoring are closely related — sanitation failures contaminate water sources. The Sanitation Service and Water Service shall share monitoring data and coordinate responses when sanitation conditions threaten water quality.

**§36. Emergency Response.** When monitoring reveals a sanitation condition that poses an immediate public health threat the Sanitation Service shall respond immediately and shall notify the Health Service, Medical College, and Assembly. Sanitation emergencies — sewage spills, waste system failures, contamination events — shall be treated with the same urgency as other public health emergencies.

---

## Article VIII — Transparency and Reporting

**§37. Annual Report.** The Sanitation Service shall publish an annual report covering: human waste system assessments and conditions in aggregate; solid waste collection volumes and disposal methods; organic waste transferred to Agricultural Service; materials recovered by type and volume; hazardous waste collected and disposed; public health monitoring findings in aggregate; and any significant developments in sanitation capacity or conditions. The report shall be presented to the Assembly and made available to all members.

**§38. Public Health Transparency.** Sanitation conditions that affect public health shall be reported to members promptly and clearly. Members have the right to know about sanitation conditions that affect their health. The Sanitation Service shall not minimize or delay reporting of public health concerns.

**§39. Collection Schedule Publication.** Current collection schedules and drop-off locations for waste, organic material, recyclables, and hazardous waste shall be published and made available to all members. Members shall always know when and how to manage their waste responsibly.
