# The Ben Franklin Society
## Of [County Name]

### Foundational Document on the Agricultural Service
*Article IX of the Bylaws*

---

## Preamble

A community that grows its own food is a community with genuine sovereignty. The Ben Franklin Society recognizes that food security begins not in the kitchen or the pantry but in the soil — in the health of the land, the knowledge of its farmers, the quality of its seed, and the collective capacity to bring harvests in. The Agricultural Service exists to build and sustain that capacity. It does not own the farms. It does not command the farmers. It serves them — with knowledge, support, coordination, and the institutional weight of a community that understands its own survival depends on their success.

---

## Article I — Nature and Mandate

**§1. Core Purpose.** The Agricultural Service exists to build, sustain, and continuously develop the community's agricultural capacity. It is in the fundamental interest of the Ben Franklin Society to grow as much of its own food as possible. The Agricultural Service is the institution through which the community pursues that interest — not through compulsion but through knowledge, support, encouragement, and coordination.

**§2. Private Farms.** The Society's farms are privately owned. The Agricultural Service does not own, manage, or control private farmland. It serves the farmers who own and work that land. This service relationship is the foundation of the Agricultural Service's mandate — it exists to make farmers more successful, not to substitute for their judgment or ownership.

**§3. Functions.** The Agricultural Service carries out its mandate through the following functions: agricultural capacity assessment; farm health support; seed library operation; farmer knowledge and training; harvest labor coordination; equipment lending; production contract support; and inter-society agricultural coordination.

**§4. Long-Term Orientation.** Agricultural capacity is built over years and decades, not seasons. Soil health, perennial systems, seed adaptation, and farmer knowledge are long-term investments. The Assembly shall recognize this in its appropriations and planning, understanding that agricultural investment today may not yield returns for years and that the absence of such investment will be felt for generations.

**§5. Oversight.** The Agricultural Service is overseen by the Agricultural College. The Agricultural College also oversees the Food Service. The College shall actively facilitate the coordination between the two services that is essential to the community's food sovereignty.

---

## Article II — Agricultural Capacity Assessment

**§6. Assessment Mandate.** The Agricultural Service shall maintain a continuous and current assessment of the community's agricultural capacity — what land is being farmed, what land could be farmed, the health and productivity of active farmland, and the trajectory of the community's growing capacity over time. You cannot build what you have not mapped.

**§7. Land Assessment.** The Agricultural Service shall assess the agricultural potential of land within the community's area — soil types, water availability, slope, sun exposure, and current use. Assessment is conducted on a voluntary basis with the cooperation of landowners. The goal is a comprehensive picture of what the community's land can produce.

**§8. Farm Health.** The Agricultural Service shall assess the health of active farms — soil nutrient levels, erosion, compaction, pest pressure, water management, and overall productivity. Farm health assessment is offered as a service to farmers, not as an inspection or regulatory function. The Agricultural Service serves farmers; it does not surveil them.

**§9. Idle Land.** The Agricultural Service shall identify land within the community's area that is capable of agricultural production but currently idle. It shall work with the Agricultural College to develop strategies for bringing productive idle land into cultivation, through farmer support, equipment access, and such other means as are available.

**§10. Reporting.** The Agricultural Service shall report its capacity assessments to the Assembly annually and to the Food Service on a schedule sufficient to inform nutritional planning. Assessment data shall inform Assembly decisions on agricultural priorities and resource allocation.

---

## Article III — Farm Support

**§11. Support Mandate.** The Agricultural Service shall provide practical support to farmers within the community on matters of soil health, water management, irrigation, pest and disease management, crop selection, and such other agricultural matters as farmers need assistance with. This is the core service relationship between the Agricultural Service and the community's farmers.

**§12. Soil Health.** Soil health is the foundation of all agricultural productivity. The Agricultural Service shall maintain deep expertise in soil science and shall make that expertise available to farmers through direct consultation, soil testing, amendment recommendations, and cover crop and rotation planning. Building soil health is among the highest-value long-term investments the community can make in its agricultural future.

**§13. Water and Irrigation.** The Agricultural Service shall provide support on water management and irrigation — assessing water availability, advising on irrigation system design and maintenance, and helping farmers manage water resources sustainably. Water security is agricultural security.

**§14. Pest and Disease Management.** The Agricultural Service shall provide support on pest and disease management, including identification, prevention, and treatment options consistent with long-term soil and ecosystem health. The Agricultural Service shall develop community-scale knowledge of local pest and disease pressures so that no farmer faces these challenges without informed support.

**§15. Perennial Systems.** The Agricultural Service shall actively encourage and support the development of perennial food systems — orchards, food forests, perennial vegetables, and such other productive perennial plantings as are suited to the community's climate and soils. Perennial systems build long-term food security with diminishing annual labor inputs. They are a multigenerational investment in community resilience.

---

## Article IV — The Seed Library

**§16. Establishment and Purpose.** The Agricultural Service shall maintain at least one seed library of open-pollinated, locally-adapted seed varieties. The seed library is among the most foundational expressions of community food sovereignty. A community that controls its own seed stock is not dependent on external systems for the origin of its food supply. The library shall be actively developed — not merely preserved — as a living repository of agricultural genetic heritage suited to the community's specific conditions.

**§17. Open-Pollinated Varieties.** The seed library shall focus on open-pollinated varieties that farmers can save, replant, and select over time. Hybrid and proprietary seed varieties that cannot be saved create ongoing dependency on external suppliers. The community's seed sovereignty depends on maintaining the ability to produce its own seed indefinitely.

**§18. Local Adaptation.** The seed library shall prioritize varieties adapted to the community's specific climate, soils, and growing conditions. Over time the Agricultural Service shall develop locally-selected strains through farmer collaboration and deliberate selection. Locally-adapted seed outperforms commercial varieties under local conditions and cannot be replicated by any external source.

**§19. Access.** Seeds from the library shall be accessible to member farmers on terms established in the standing rules. Farmers who grow out library varieties are expected to return a portion of saved seed to the library, maintaining and replenishing its stock. The library operates on a reciprocal basis consistent with the Society's mutual aid principles.

**§20. Inter-Society Coordination.** The seed library shall coordinate with the seed libraries of other Ben Franklin Societies to share varieties, exchange locally-adapted strains, and maintain genetic diversity across the federation network. A networked seed library system is far more resilient than any single library.

---

## Article V — Farmer Knowledge and Training

**§21. Knowledge Mandate.** Agricultural knowledge is community infrastructure. The Agricultural Service shall actively develop, preserve, and transmit farming knowledge within the community. The goal is a community in which agricultural knowledge is held broadly — not concentrated in a few practitioners but distributed across a large body of knowledgeable members.

**§22. New Farmer Development.** The Agricultural Service shall actively support and encourage new farmers — members who wish to begin farming but lack experience, land access, or resources. Expanding the number of active farmers in the community directly expands the community's food sovereignty. The Agricultural Service shall develop programs to lower the barriers to beginning farming.

**§23. Training Programs.** The Agricultural Service shall maintain ongoing training programs in practical farming skills — soil management, crop production, water management, pest control, seed saving, food preservation, and such other skills as the Agricultural College determines relevant. Training shall be accessible to all members regardless of current farming experience.

**§24. Knowledge Transmission.** The Agricultural Service shall ensure that the agricultural knowledge held by experienced farmers in the community is transmitted to newer ones. Experienced farmer knowledge — of local soils, local weather patterns, local pest cycles, local crop varieties — is irreplaceable and cannot be recovered once lost. The Agricultural Service shall treat its transmission as a priority.

---

## Article VI — Harvest Labor Coordination

**§25. Harvest Mandate.** Harvest is time-critical in a way that most agricultural work is not. Crops that cannot be brought in within a narrow window are lost. The Agricultural Service shall coordinate community labor for harvest across farms, mobilizing members to assist farmers when harvest demands exceed what a single farm's household can provide. This is mutual aid in one of its oldest and most essential forms.

**§26. Coordination System.** The Agricultural Service shall maintain a harvest labor coordination system through which farmers can request community labor assistance and members can participate in harvest work across the community's farms. *[Placeholder: labor coordination system design and compensation arrangements to be established in standing rules.]*

**§27. Reciprocity.** Harvest labor coordination operates on a mutual aid basis. Farmers who receive labor assistance from the community are expected to contribute labor to other farms and to the community's agricultural work. The Agricultural Service shall maintain records of labor exchange sufficient to ensure the system remains genuinely reciprocal.

**§28. Beyond Harvest.** While harvest is the most time-sensitive occasion for labor coordination, the Agricultural Service may coordinate community labor for other agricultural purposes — planting, infrastructure installation, emergency response to crop threats — as the need arises.

---

## Article VII — Equipment Lending

**§29. Purpose.** Agricultural equipment is expensive and many pieces of equipment are needed only seasonally. The Society may establish an equipment lending program to provide member farmers with access to equipment they could not individually afford or justify owning. Shared equipment access lowers the barrier to farming and increases the productive capacity of the community's agricultural base.

**§30. Community Equipment.** The community does not mandate equipment ownership but may acquire and lend equipment as the Assembly determines appropriate. Equipment held by the Agricultural Service for lending is community property maintained for community benefit. *[Placeholder: equipment lending terms, maintenance responsibilities, and scheduling to be established in standing rules.]*

**§31. Maintenance.** Community equipment shall be maintained in good working condition. The Agricultural Service is responsible for equipment maintenance and shall establish protocols ensuring that equipment is returned in the condition it was lent and that maintenance is performed on schedule.

---

## Article VIII — Production Contract Support

**§32. Contract System.** The Society maintains a production contract system through which farmers agree to grow specified crops in specified quantities for the community's food supply. This system is addressed in a separate foundational document. The Agricultural Service participates in the contract system as the primary point of contact between the community and its farmers.

**§33. Farmer Support.** The Agricultural Service shall support farmers in understanding, negotiating, and fulfilling their production contracts. Contracts are tools of community food planning — they work best when farmers understand them fully and are supported in meeting them. The Agricultural Service is the farmer's advocate and support in the contract system.

**§34. Data Sharing.** The Agricultural Service shall share production assessment and contract data with the Food Service on a schedule sufficient to support nutritional planning. The contract system connects the community's food needs to actual growing decisions — that connection depends on timely and accurate data flowing between the two services.

---

## Article IX — Encouragement of Local Food Production

**§35. Community Interest.** It is in the fundamental interest of the Ben Franklin Society to grow as much of its own food as possible. A community that feeds itself is a community that is genuinely resilient. The Agricultural Service is charged with actively encouraging local food production — not through compulsion but by making farming as accessible, supported, and rewarding as possible.

**§36. Member Growing.** The Agricultural Service shall encourage and support food growing by members beyond commercial farming — home gardens, community growing spaces, small-scale production. Every additional source of food within the community reduces its dependence on external supply chains. The Agricultural Service shall make knowledge and support available to all members who wish to grow food regardless of scale.

**§37. Reducing External Dependence.** The Agricultural Service shall track the community's dependence on external food sources and shall report to the Assembly on progress toward greater food self-sufficiency. Reducing external food dependence is a long-term goal that requires sustained institutional attention and investment.

---

## Article X — Inter-Society Agricultural Coordination

**§38. Network.** The Agricultural Service shall maintain active relationships with the agricultural services of other Ben Franklin Societies. Agricultural knowledge, seed varieties, labor in exceptional circumstances, and production data are all resources that benefit from sharing across the federation network.

**§39. Knowledge Exchange.** The Agricultural Service shall actively exchange agricultural knowledge with other societies — what works in local conditions, what varieties are performing well, what pest and disease pressures are emerging. Community-scale agricultural knowledge networks are more responsive and locally relevant than any centralized extension system.

**§40. Seed Network.** Inter-society seed library coordination is addressed in Article IV. The Agricultural Service shall treat seed network relationships as among its most important inter-society functions.

---

## Article XI — Transparency and Reporting

**§41. Annual Report.** The Agricultural Service shall publish an annual report covering: agricultural capacity assessment results; farm health summary; seed library holdings and activity; farmer support engagements; training program participation; harvest labor coordination activity; equipment lending utilization; production contract summary in coordination with the Food Service; and inter-society coordination activity. The report shall be presented to the Assembly and made available to all members.

**§42. Food Sovereignty Metric.** The annual report shall include a community food sovereignty metric — an estimate of what percentage of the community's food needs are currently being met by local production.
