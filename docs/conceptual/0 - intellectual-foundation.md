Premise one: We are past the point of sustainable complexity

Tainter's framework applied to the present moment. The marginal return on complexity investment has gone negative across multiple critical systems simultaneously. The complexity will fall. The question is what catches it.
This premise explains why the Ben Franklin Society isn't reform. You're not trying to fix the hospital system or the political system or the financial system. You're building at a different complexity level that can sustain itself when those systems simplify. The scaffolding is not the building. It catches what's worth catching during the transition.

Premise two: The average American is a good person who, given time and structured deliberation with their peers, can almost always make well-reasoned, nuanced, and thoughtful collective decisions

This is the premise that the entire current political system is designed to deny.
The political system as currently structured operates on the opposite assumption — that ordinary people cannot be trusted to make good decisions, that they need to be managed, that their role is to choose between options presented to them by people who know better, and that genuine deliberation is either impossible or dangerous.
This assumption is self-serving and self-fulfilling. It's self-serving because it justifies the concentration of decision-making authority in a class of political professionals. It's self-fulfilling because a system designed around the assumption that people can't govern themselves produces people who have forgotten how to govern themselves.
The research on deliberative democracy tells a different story. When ordinary people are given time, accurate information, genuine deliberation with their peers, and real decision-making authority — they consistently produce thoughtful, nuanced, reasonable outcomes. The Irish Citizens' Assembly on abortion. The British Columbia Citizens' Assembly on electoral reform. The deliberative polls developed by James Fishkin. Every serious experiment in genuine deliberative democracy has produced the same result: ordinary people, given the right conditions, govern well.
Not perfectly. Not without disagreement. But thoughtfully. Considering evidence. Changing their minds when the evidence warrants it. Finding genuine common ground across political and cultural differences.

One: The complexity is unsustainable and falling.
Two: Government legitimacy requires the consent of the governed and we don't have that in any meaningful way.
Three: Ordinary people given structured deliberation make good decisions.
Four: We shouldn't abandon the current system but we can't rely exclusively on it any longer.
