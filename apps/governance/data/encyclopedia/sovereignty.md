# Sovereignty

## What It Is

Sovereignty is supreme authority - the ultimate power to make decisions and establish rules within a territory or political community. It is the answer to the question: "Who has the right to rule?"

The term derives from the Latin *superanus*, meaning "above" or "supreme." The sovereign is the highest authority, subject to no other power. All other authorities derive their legitimacy from the sovereign, but the sovereign derives legitimacy from no higher source.

Historically, sovereignty could reside in different places:
- A monarch (divine right kingship)
- An aristocratic body (oligarchy)
- The people collectively (popular sovereignty)
- A constitution or fundamental law

The critical question in political philosophy has always been: Who is sovereign? Who has the ultimate right to decide?

## History

**Medieval sovereignty:**

In medieval Europe, sovereignty was understood to reside in monarchs who ruled by divine right. Kings and queens claimed their authority came from God, not from the consent of the people. The monarch was sovereign - the ultimate earthly authority, answerable only to God.

This theory faced challenges from:
- The Catholic Church, which claimed spiritual authority over temporal rulers
- Feudal nobles, who exercised considerable autonomy within their domains
- Growing merchant classes who wanted representation

**Social contract theory:**

Enlightenment philosophers fundamentally challenged divine right sovereignty:

**Thomas Hobbes** (*Leviathan*, 1651): Argued that people in a "state of nature" would live in constant fear and violence. To escape this, they entered a social contract, surrendering their natural liberty to a sovereign (monarch or assembly) who would maintain order. Once established, the sovereign's power was absolute. People could not take it back without returning to the state of nature.

**John Locke** (*Two Treatises of Government*, 1689): Argued that sovereignty ultimately resided with the people. They created government through social contract to protect their natural rights (life, liberty, property). If government violated these rights, people retained the right to dissolve it and establish new government. Sovereignty wasn't permanently surrendered - it was conditionally delegated.

**Jean-Jacques Rousseau** (*The Social Contract*, 1762): Distinguished between sovereignty (the general will of the people) and government (the executive body that implements the general will). Sovereignty was inalienable and indivisible - it could not be transferred or divided. The people were sovereign; government officials were merely servants executing the sovereign will. Representatives could not make law; only the assembled people could express the general will.

**Popular sovereignty in practice:**

The American and French revolutions attempted to instantiate popular sovereignty:

**American Revolution (1776)**: The Declaration of Independence stated that governments derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed" and that when government becomes destructive of people's rights, "it is the Right of the People to alter or to abolish it."

However, the U.S. Constitution (1787) created a complex system:
- "We the People" claimed sovereign authority in the Preamble
- But the people didn't govern directly - they elected representatives
- Representatives weren't bound delegates; they exercised independent judgment
- Constitutional amendment required supermajorities, making fundamental change difficult
- Numerous anti-majoritarian institutions (Electoral College, Senate, judicial review) checked popular will

**French Revolution (1789)**: The Declaration of the Rights of Man declared that "the principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation." But in practice, this meant the National Assembly claimed to represent the nation's sovereignty, not that citizens exercised sovereignty directly.

**The representative compromise:**

Most modern "democracies" adopted representative government with periodic elections. This created theoretical tension:

- If the people are sovereign, why can't they make laws directly?
- If representatives make laws, aren't *they* effectively sovereign?
- If people only vote every few years for representatives who then act independently, is this really popular sovereignty or just aristocracy with democratic aesthetics?

Rousseau was blunt: "The English people believes itself to be free; it is gravely mistaken; it is free only during the election of Members of Parliament; as soon as the Members are elected, the people is enslaved; it is nothing."

**20th-21st century erosion:**

By the early 21st century, even the claim of popular sovereignty through representation faced serious challenges:

- **Oligarchic capture**: Studies suggested that policy outcomes reflected elite and corporate preferences rather than median voter preferences
- **Money in politics**: Campaign finance and lobbying concentrated political influence among the wealthy
- **Partisan control**: Party leadership, not voters, often determined which candidates appeared on ballots and what legislation reached votes
- **Bureaucratic authority**: Unelected administrative agencies made most "laws" through rulemaking
- **International institutions**: Trade agreements and international bodies constrained national sovereignty, often limiting democratic control

The practical location of sovereignty became unclear. The people voted, but did they rule?

## In the Benjamin Franklin Society

The BFS takes popular sovereignty seriously as the foundational principle of legitimate governance: the members collectively are sovereign, and sovereignty is inalienable.

### Sovereignty Resides in the Membership

Ultimate authority rests with the members collectively, not with any representative body, official, or institution. The General Assembly, committees, officers, and all other structures exist to serve the sovereign membership. They exercise delegated authority, not sovereignty itself.

### Inalienable Sovereignty

Following Rousseau, the BFS holds that sovereignty cannot be permanently transferred or surrendered. When members create governance structures, they delegate authority for specific purposes, but they retain sovereignty.

This means:
- No representative body can alter the fundamental constitution without member approval
- Members can petition and vote directly on laws and policies
- The sovereign membership can overrule any decision by delegated authorities
- Governance structures exist at the pleasure of the sovereign membership

### Active Sovereignty

Sovereignty is not passive or theoretical - it is actively exercised through direct mechanisms:

**[[Referendum]]**: Direct votes on constitutional amendments, major policies, and questions referred by the General Assembly. When members vote in referendum, they are exercising sovereignty directly.

**[[Petition]]**: Any member can initiate legislative action by petition. Upon reaching the signature threshold, the proposal goes to referendum for sovereign decision. This ensures sovereignty isn't monopolized by those selected for office.

**Constitutional supremacy**: The charter (constitution) is the expression of sovereign will. It can only be amended by referendum, ensuring that fundamental rules require direct sovereign approval.

### Delegated Authority Through Sortition

The General Assembly and other sortition-based bodies exercise *delegated authority*, not sovereignty. They are not representatives who substitute their judgment for the people's will - they are randomly-selected citizens temporarily entrusted with specific governance functions.

Key distinctions from traditional representatives:

**Limited tenure**: Short, non-renewable terms ensure that those exercising delegated authority return to ordinary citizenship and live under the rules they helped create.

**Random selection**: [[Sortition]] ensures that authority isn't captured by a professional political class. Any member might be selected, reinforcing that these are peers exercising temporary authority, not a separate ruling class.

**Subject to referendum**: Major decisions can be referred to the full membership. Sortition bodies cannot claim to exclusively represent the sovereign will.

**Constitutional constraints**: Delegated authorities operate within bounds set by the sovereign membership through the constitution.

### Continuous Sovereignty

Popular sovereignty is not a one-time event at founding - it is continuous. The members don't exercise sovereignty once when creating the society and then hand control to representatives. They continuously participate in governance through:

- Regular referenda on constitutional and legislative questions
- Ongoing ability to petition for changes
- Participation in deliberation and discussion
- Potential selection for sortition service

The sovereign people don't delegate sovereignty and then step back - they remain actively sovereign, using both direct mechanisms and temporary delegated authorities as tools of self-governance.

### Rationale

Taking sovereignty seriously shapes the entire governance structure:

**Why referenda**: Because the sovereign people have the right to decide fundamental questions directly, not merely elect others to decide for them.

**Why petition**: Because any citizen must have the power to bring questions before the sovereign assembly (the full membership) for decision.

**Why sortition**: Because delegated authority should be temporary, rotated broadly, and prevent formation of a ruling class separate from the sovereign people.

**Why constitutional constraint**: Because even sovereign majorities should not easily violate fundamental principles - the constitution embodies considered sovereign judgment that shouldn't be overturned by momentary passions.

**Why short terms**: Because those exercising delegated authority should remain accountable and return to life as ordinary citizens under the rules they helped create.

The BFS rejects the notion that popular sovereignty means occasional elections where people choose rulers. It insists that sovereignty means the people govern themselves directly, using both direct mechanisms and carefully limited delegated authorities.

The members are not electors choosing rulers. They are not constituents represented by officials. They are co-equal citizens exercising collective sovereignty over their common affairs.

### Sovereignty as Voluntary Association

It is important to clarify what BFS sovereignty means and does not mean:

**What it means:**
- Members collectively govern their internal affairs and relationships with each other
- The society establishes its own rules, procedures, and institutions for self-governance
- Members voluntarily submit to the authority of decisions made through legitimate BFS processes
- The society exercises autonomy over its internal organization, similar to how corporations, religious organizations, and other voluntary associations govern themselves

**What it does not mean:**
- BFS does not claim territorial sovereignty or jurisdiction over geographic areas
- BFS does not claim legal supremacy over or independence from the United States government
- Members remain citizens of the United States subject to all federal, state, and local laws
- BFS sovereignty does not exempt members or the society from external legal obligations
- The society operates within the legal framework of the jurisdictions where it exists

This understanding of sovereignty has ample precedent:
- Native American tribes exercise "tribal sovereignty" over internal affairs while existing within US jurisdiction
- Corporations establish internal governance structures and rules for shareholders and employees
- Religious organizations maintain internal authority, doctrines, and procedures
- Labor unions govern their internal affairs and member relationships
- Homeowners associations establish rules for members

BFS sovereignty is the sovereignty of a voluntary association over its internal governance - the right of members to collectively determine how they will organize themselves, make decisions together, and structure their relationships with each other. It is not a claim to territorial independence or legal immunity.

Members who violate US law remain subject to legal consequences regardless of BFS rules. If US law conflicts with BFS practices, members must comply with US law. The society exists within, not outside or above, the legal order.

What BFS sovereignty does assert is that within the bounds of law, the members - not external authorities, not corporate boards, not appointed officials - collectively govern their association. The state doesn't dictate BFS internal procedures any more than it dictates corporate bylaws or church governance structures.
