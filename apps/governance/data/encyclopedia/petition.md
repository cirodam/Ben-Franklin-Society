# Petition

## What It Is

A petition is a formal written request to an authority, asking for specific action, policy change, or redress of grievances. The term derives from the Latin *petitio*, meaning "a request" or "seeking."

Traditionally, petitions were submitted by subjects or citizens to those holding power - monarchs, legislatures, officials - asking them to grant relief, change policy, or address complaints. The petition represented an appeal from below to those above in a hierarchical system.

The right to petition was considered fundamental to limited government: even if subjects couldn't vote or hold office, they could at least ask authorities to address their concerns. Authorities might ignore petitions, but the right to submit them established that power had some duty to listen.

## History

**Medieval origins:**

Petitioning developed in medieval Europe as a formal mechanism for subjects to request royal favor or justice:
- Magna Carta (1215) was itself a petition from English barons to King John, demanding specific rights and limitations on royal power
- Medieval parliaments often began as occasions for subjects to present petitions to the monarch
- Petitions could request anything from tax relief to resolution of disputes to changes in law

**English constitutional development:**

The right to petition became enshrined in English constitutional law:
- The English Bill of Rights (1689) declared "that it is the right of the subjects to petition the King, and all commitments and prosecutions for such petitioning are illegal"
- This established that subjects couldn't be punished for requesting action from government
- Parliament handled thousands of petitions annually on matters ranging from local concerns to national policy

**American tradition:**

The United States Constitution's First Amendment (1791) guaranteed "the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances."

- Congress received countless petitions on issues like abolition of slavery, women's suffrage, prohibition, and war
- The gag rule (1836-1844) prohibiting discussion of anti-slavery petitions was deeply controversial, seen as violating constitutional rights
- Petition campaigns were important tools for political movements denied other forms of power

**Modern transformation:**

By the late 20th century, petitions had evolved in different directions:

**Online symbolic petitions**: Platforms like Change.org, MoveOn, and others made it easy to create and sign petitions on any topic. However, these were typically symbolic gestures with no legal force - authorities could simply ignore them. The petition became less a formal mechanism and more a form of public opinion expression.

**Ballot initiative petitions**: Many U.S. states developed petition processes for placing initiatives on the ballot:
- Citizens drafted a proposed law or constitutional amendment
- Gathered signatures (typically a percentage of voters in the previous election)
- If the signature threshold was met, the measure appeared on the ballot for direct vote
- This transformed the petition from a request to authority into a mechanism for direct lawmaking

However, ballot initiative petitions often required substantial resources. Professional signature-gathering firms charged per signature, giving wealthy interests advantages. Some measures were backed by hidden funders using petition processes to circumvent legislatures.

**Contemporary problems:**

Traditional petitions to representatives often went nowhere. Officials might receive thousands of petitions but faced no obligation to act. Citizens experienced petitions as performative exercises - they could voice concerns, but authorities could ignore them. The right to petition became the right to be ignored politely.

## In the Benjamin Franklin Society

The BFS transforms the petition from a request to authority into a mechanism for direct legislative action by citizens.

### Petition as Citizen Initiative

In BFS, a petition is not a request for someone else to act - it is a proposal that citizens themselves will vote on. When members petition, they're not asking the General Assembly or any other body to do something; they're initiating a [[Referendum]] that all members will decide.

**The process:**
1. A member drafts a proposal (constitutional amendment, new law, policy change, repeal of existing law)
2. The proposal is published in a public registry where all members can review it
3. Other members may add their signatures in support
4. Upon reaching the signature threshold, the proposal automatically advances to referendum
5. All members vote directly on the proposal
6. If approved by the required majority, the proposal becomes law

No representative body can block a petition that meets the threshold. No official can decide the question isn't important enough. If citizens support bringing the question to a vote, it goes to a vote.

### Signature Threshold

The signature requirement serves several purposes:

**Demonstrates seriousness**: Requiring support from some portion of the membership (e.g., 10%) ensures that proposals advancing to referendum have meaningful backing, not just one person's whim.

**Encourages deliberation**: To gather signatures, the proposer must persuade fellow citizens that the question merits consideration. This creates initial deliberative discussion before the referendum.

**Filters frivolous proposals**: The threshold prevents the referendum process from being overwhelmed with trivial or absurd proposals.

**Builds momentum**: Successful signature-gathering creates awareness and discussion of the proposal before the vote.

### Open to All Members

Any member can initiate a petition. There is no requirement to hold office, belong to a committee, or have special status. A new member and a founding member have equal standing to propose changes.

This ensures that governance is not monopolized by sortition bodies or experienced members. If any citizen believes something should change and can persuade enough fellow citizens to agree, that question goes before the entire membership.

### No Gatekeepers

Unlike systems where petitions ask representatives to act, BFS petitions bypass representative discretion entirely. The General Assembly cannot prevent a petition from advancing to referendum (though they might offer recommendations on how to vote).

This direct path from citizen proposal to referendum to law means members don't need permission from anyone to change their society - they need only to persuade their fellow citizens.

### Rationale

The BFS petition process serves democratic empowerment:

**Citizen sovereignty**: Members aren't subjects petitioning authorities; they are co-equal citizens proposing actions to their peers.

**Initiative, not request**: Petitions initiate action rather than requesting it. The power lies with the citizenry, not representatives who might grant or deny requests.

**Corrects institutional failures**: If sortition bodies, committees, or existing structures fail to address an issue, any citizen can force consideration through petition.

**Continuous constitution-making**: The petition process means the society's fundamental rules and laws are never fixed permanently. Citizens continuously reshape their institutions based on experience.

**Political equality**: Every member has equal standing to propose changes, regardless of tenure, social standing, or expertise.

The petition is thus not a humble request to power, but an assertion of popular sovereignty - citizens exercising their authority to govern themselves directly.
