# Mutual Aid

## What It Is

Mutual aid was the practice of reciprocal exchange of resources, services, and support within communities, based on voluntary cooperation rather than market transactions or hierarchical charity. Members of mutual aid societies or informal networks helped each other through pooled resources and shared labor, creating systems of mutual support outside both market and state.

Mutual aid was distinguished from charity by its reciprocal nature - not helping the unfortunate from above, but members supporting each other as equals. It was distinguished from market exchange by its non-transactional character - not buying and selling services, but creating ongoing relationships of mutual support where help flowed based on need rather than payment.

The core principle: people facing similar circumstances band together to help each other, pooling resources and sharing risks. When one member needs help, others provide it, knowing they may need help themselves someday. Reciprocity without strict accounting, solidarity through shared vulnerability.

**Key features:**

**Voluntary association**: People chose to join mutual aid societies or networks. Not imposed from above, but organized from below by participants themselves.

**Reciprocal support**: Help flowed in multiple directions. Not benefactors helping beneficiaries, but members supporting each other.

**Pooled resources**: Sharing risks and resources - sick benefits, funeral expenses, unemployment support, childcare, tools, labor, knowledge.

**Solidarity, not transaction**: Relationships based on mutual commitment, not individual transactions. Helping because we're in this together, not because you paid.

**Horizontal, not hierarchical**: Members as equals supporting each other, not clients receiving services from providers or poor receiving charity from wealthy.

**Beyond the nuclear family**: Extended networks of mutual support beyond immediate family - neighborhood, occupation, ethnic community, ideological affinity.

Mutual aid created thick webs of interdependence and solidarity, enabling people to weather hardships together that they couldn't face alone.

## History

**Ancient and medieval practices:**

Mutual aid practices existed across cultures and eras, though often unnamed:

**Village commons**: Shared grazing lands, forests, water sources. Community management of resources for mutual benefit.

**Guilds**: Medieval craftspeople organized mutual support - regulating trade, maintaining quality standards, supporting sick or injured members, training apprentices, burying dead.

**Barn raising and work bees**: Neighbors gathering to build barns, harvest crops, quilting bees. Reciprocal labor exchange.

**Extended kinship networks**: Family and clan systems providing mutual support across generations and branches.

These weren't formalized "mutual aid societies" but reflected the principle: people supporting each other through reciprocal relationships.

**Friendly societies and mutual aid societies:**

By the 18th-19th centuries in Britain and North America, working-class people formalized mutual aid through friendly societies (Britain) or mutual aid societies (US):

**Structure**: Workers paid regular dues into common fund. When members fell sick, injured, unemployed, or died, fund provided benefits - medical care, income support, funeral expenses.

**Scale**: Massive participation. By 1900s, more than half of British working-class men belonged to friendly societies. In US, large proportion of workers belonged to mutual aid societies, fraternal organizations, or ethnic benevolent associations.

**Self-governance**: Members elected officers, set dues and benefits, made decisions collectively. Not charitable institutions run by elites, but working-class self-organization.

**Beyond economic**: Societies provided social life - meetings, celebrations, rituals, identity. Not just insurance schemes but communities.

**Ethnic and occupational**: Different societies for different groups - Irish, Italian, Jewish mutual aid societies; railroad workers, miners, particular trades. Shared identity strengthened solidarity.

**Women's societies**: Though often excluded from male societies, women organized their own mutual aid networks - particularly around childbirth, childcare, sick care, death rituals.

These societies were the primary form of social insurance for working people before the welfare state. They demonstrated that ordinary people could organize mutual support effectively without relying on charity or state.

**Labor unions as mutual aid:**

Labor unions functioned partly as mutual aid societies:
- Strike funds supporting workers during disputes
- Death benefits for members' families  
- Sick benefits, unemployment support
- Educational programs, social activities

The "mutual benefit association" combined union organizing with mutual aid functions.

**Kropotkin's evolutionary theory:**

Peter Kropotkin's *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution* (1902) argued against social Darwinism's "survival of the fittest":

**Cooperation, not just competition**: Evolution favored species that cooperated. Mutual aid was as important as competition for survival.

**Human societies**: Throughout history, human societies survived through mutual aid - communal hunting, shared resources, collective defense, reciprocal support.

**Against Malthus**: Kropotkin rejected Malthusian competition for scarce resources as primary driver. Humans thrived by helping each other, not just competing.

**Anarchist vision**: Mutual aid demonstrated that humans could organize cooperation voluntarily, without state or market coercion. Foundation for anarchist communism.

Whether you accept Kropotkin's evolutionary claims or anarchist politics, his work documented extensive mutual aid practices and argued for their centrality to human life.

**20th century decline:**

Mutual aid societies declined dramatically in the 20th century:

**Welfare state expansion**: Government took over functions mutual aid societies performed - Social Security (1935), Medicare/Medicaid (1960s), unemployment insurance, disability benefits. State provided what societies once had.

**Commercial insurance**: Insurance companies offered products competing with mutual aid - life insurance, health insurance, disability insurance. Professional services replaced mutual support.

**Labor law changes**: Wagner Act (1935) formalized union recognition but also bureaucratized them, reducing mutual aid functions in favor of collective bargaining focus.

**Geographic mobility**: People moving for work disrupted stable communities where mutual aid thrived. Harder to maintain reciprocal relationships when people constantly moved.

**Individualization**: Cultural shift toward nuclear family independence, professional services, market solutions. Mutual aid seemed old-fashioned, inefficient, parochial.

By late 20th century, mutual aid societies mostly disappeared. What survived were often empty shells - fraternal organizations that were once mutual aid societies but became mainly social clubs.

**Modern resurgence:**

Late 20th and early 21st centuries saw renewed interest:

**Community support networks**: Informal mutual aid networks for childcare, eldercare, food sharing, tool libraries, time banks, skill sharing.

**Disaster response**: Hurricane Katrina, COVID-19 pandemic, various disasters prompted mutual aid networks - neighbors helping neighbors when official systems failed.

**Political movements**: Anarchist, socialist, and progressive movements revived mutual aid as political practice - creating alternative institutions and demonstrating solidarity.

**Critique of the state**: Both left (state too bureaucratic, impersonal) and right (state too coercive, inefficient) rediscovered mutual aid as alternative to state provision.

**Online platforms**: Technology enabled new forms of mutual aid - crowdfunding medical expenses, meal trains, community pantries, buy nothing groups.

However, these modern versions often remained marginal - supplements to state/market, not replacements. And many lacked the stable, ongoing relationships of historical mutual aid societies.

## In the Benjamin Franklin Society

The BFS recovers mutual aid as a central organizing principle for economic and social life.

### Mutual Aid as Economic Foundation

Mutual aid in BFS is not merely a social practice alongside the economy - it IS the foundational economic system for necessities.

**Provisioning through mutual aid**: The basic needs for human flourishing - healthcare, food, housing, childcare, education, eldercare, disability support - are provisioned through mutual aid rather than market exchange. Members contribute their capacities through [[Service]] and receive what they need through reciprocal support.

**Not the entire economy**: Beyond these necessities, market elements exist. Crafts, specialized goods, non-essential items, particular services - these can be exchanged through markets where appropriate. But markets are not the foundation; mutual aid is.

**Hybrid model**: The economic system is hybrid:
- **Mutual aid layer**: Foundation ensuring everyone's basic needs are met through reciprocal relationships, pooled resources, and contribution of capacities
- **Market layer**: For goods and services beyond basic provisioning, where exchange makes sense

This aligns with [[Oikonomia]] - economy as household management for sufficiency. Mutual aid handles the provisioning economy (the necessities), leaving space for market activity without letting markets dominate or determine whether people's basic needs are met.

**Why this matters**: In pure market economies, access to necessities depends on ability to pay. Those without means go without healthcare, food security, housing. In pure command economies, bureaucracies allocate necessities through formal procedures.

Mutual aid as foundation means: your basic needs are met through being part of a community of mutual support, not through purchasing power or bureaucratic qualification. Beyond that foundation, markets can function without creating deprivation.

### Community Banks as Mutual Aid Societies

[[Community Bank]]s function as modern mutual aid societies:

**Pooled savings**: Members deposit savings into common pool. Not investing for maximum return, but creating resource available for members' needs.

**Mutual credit**: Members can borrow from pool at low or zero interest. Not commercial lending for profit, but mutual support - helping members meet needs.

**Shared risk**: By pooling resources, individual hardships don't create crises. Community absorbs shocks together.

**Democratic governance**: Members decide collectively how bank operates - what kinds of loans to support, how to handle difficulties, what policies to adopt.

**Beyond transaction**: Banking relationships become mutual aid relationships. Not customer and bank, but members supporting each other.

This recovers the friendly society model: working people pooling resources to support each other, outside commercial financial system.

### Service System as Mutual Aid

The society's [[Service]] system embodies mutual aid principles:

**Contributing capacities**: Members offer what they can do - healthcare, childcare, education, construction, food production, eldercare.

**Receiving based on need**: Members access services based on need, not ability to pay. Need healthcare? Healthcare College provides it. Need childcare? Childcare Service provides it.

**Not market exchange**: No one calculates whether they're "getting their money's worth." You contribute what you can; you receive what you need. Reciprocity without accounting.

**Not charity**: Receivers aren't supplicants receiving help from benefactors. Everyone both contributes (through their service) and receives (services they need). Mutual aid, not charity.

**Solidarity through participation**: Everyone participates in the system of mutual support. Creates sense of "we're in this together" rather than "us helping them."

This is mutual aid at societal scale - entire community organized around reciprocal support.

### Commons and Mutual Aid

The BFS emphasis on commons reflects mutual aid principles:

**Shared resources**: Land, tools, facilities, knowledge managed collectively for mutual benefit rather than individual ownership.

**Coordinated access**: Community works out how members access commons - not market allocation (who can pay) or bureaucratic allocation (who qualifies), but collective decision about mutual support.

**Maintained collectively**: Members contribute labor to maintain commons they all benefit from. Mutual responsibility, mutual benefit.

See [[Commons]] and [[Oikonomia]] for how this works.

### Colleges as Mutual Aid Networks

[[College]]s function as domain-specific mutual aid:

**Sharing expertise**: Experienced practitioners mentor newcomers, share knowledge, help others develop skills. Not market exchange (pay for instruction) but mutual aid within community of practice.

**Collective problem-solving**: When someone faces difficulty in their domain, college members help figure it out. Pool wisdom and experience for mutual benefit.

**Tool and resource sharing**: Within domains, sharing tools and resources rather than everyone buying their own. Collective ownership supporting mutual work.

**Covering for each other**: When someone is sick, injured, or needs time away, others cover. Mutual support within domain.

Colleges create thick networks of mutual support within particular areas of expertise and practice.

### Not Charity

The BFS carefully distinguishes mutual aid from charity:

**Charity is hierarchical**: Benefactors with resources helping beneficiaries without. "Us helping them." Reinforces status differences.

**Mutual aid is horizontal**: Members supporting each other as equals. Everyone is both giver and receiver at different times. "We help each other."

**Charity creates dependence**: Receivers depend on benefactors' continued generosity and good will. No control over what's offered or how.

**Mutual aid creates interdependence**: Members depend on each other and have say in how the system works. Shared vulnerability, shared power.

**Charity is discretionary**: Benefactors choose whether to give. Can withdraw support anytime.

**Mutual aid is systematic**: Members commit to supporting each other through formal or informal systems. Reliable, not discretionary.

The BFS aims for mutual aid, not charity - solidarity among equals, not beneficence from above.

### Not Market Exchange

Equally important, mutual aid differs from market transactions:

**Markets are transactional**: Discrete exchanges. You pay, you receive. Account closed. No ongoing relationship required.

**Mutual aid is relational**: Ongoing relationships of reciprocal support. Not closed transactions but continuing commitment to help each other.

**Markets are impersonal**: Don't need to know seller or buyer. Anonymous exchange based on price.

**Mutual aid is personal**: Based on being part of shared community. Know who you're helping; they know you.

**Markets are calculated**: Track what you give and receive. Expect equivalence. "Did I get my money's worth?"

**Mutual aid is uncalculated**: Reciprocity without strict accounting. Contribute what you can; receive what you need. Trust it balances over time and across community.

**Markets atomize**: Individual buyers and sellers competing. My advantage may be your disadvantage.

**Mutual aid solidifies**: We're in this together. Your wellbeing supports mine and vice versa.

The BFS economic system is based on mutual aid, not market exchange - though it recognizes some role for markets in certain contexts.

### Reciprocity Without Accounting

Key to BFS mutual aid is "reciprocity without accounting":

**Not strict exchange**: Don't track precisely whether what you give equals what you receive. No tabs, no debts (beyond Community Bank loans for specific purposes).

**Trust in balance**: Trust that over time, across community, things roughly balance. Everyone contributes what they can; everyone receives what they need; it works out.

**Avoiding exploitation**: Need ways to handle free-riders - those who take without giving back. But don't want to reduce everything to transaction and calculation.

**Community judgment**: Relies on [[Practical Wisdom]] - community judgment about who's contributing fairly versus who's exploiting. Not metrics but perception and deliberation.

**Different capacities**: Some people can contribute more (able-bodied, skilled, energetic); others less (elderly, disabled, young). Mutual aid means supporting each other despite different capacities, not calculating equivalence.

This requires trust and community cohesion - reason mutual aid works better at smaller scale where people know each other.

### Building Solidarity

Mutual aid creates solidarity - sense of being bound together in mutual commitment:

**Shared vulnerability**: Recognizing we all face hardships, uncertainties, needs. No one is completely self-sufficient.

**Interdependence**: Actually depending on each other, not just theoretically connected. My wellbeing depends on yours and vice versa.

**Equality in vulnerability**: Rich and poor, skilled and unskilled, young and old - everyone needs support sometimes. Mutual aid recognizes our shared vulnerability.

**Commitment through practice**: Solidarity isn't just feeling - it's enacted through helping each other. Practice of mutual aid builds commitment to each other.

**Against individualism**: Mutual aid counteracts modern individualism - the fantasy that we can be independent, self-sufficient, not needing others. Recognizes our fundamental interdependence.

The BFS sees solidarity as essential for human flourishing, and mutual aid as how solidarity is built and maintained.

### Scale and Boundaries

Mutual aid works best at certain scales and with certain boundaries:

**Small enough to know**: Harder to maintain reciprocal relationships in massive anonymous systems. BFS communities stay small enough that members can know each other.

**Bounded membership**: Mutual aid requires some boundary - can't support everyone equally. BFS has membership process, distinguishing members from non-members.

**Multiple scales**: Mutual aid can work at different levels - household, neighborhood, society, Federation. Different scales for different functions.

**Not universal**: Modern welfare state aimed to provide universal benefits to all citizens. Mutual aid societies historically served particular groups - workers in a trade, ethnic community, geographic area. BFS is bounded - serving members, not humanity generally.

This raises tensions: Does boundedness exclude those who need support? Is it parochial? These are real challenges the society grapples with.

### Challenges and Limitations

The BFS recognizes mutual aid isn't perfect:

**Free-rider problems**: Some people might take without giving back. Need ways to address this without destroying trust and reciprocity.

**Unequal needs**: What if some members need far more support than others? Does mutual aid break down?

**Capacity limits**: What if community faces crisis beyond its capacity to handle mutually? When do you need outside help?

**Exclusion risks**: Bounded mutual aid means some people are outside the boundary. What responsibility to non-members?

**Nostalgia danger**: Is this romanticizing the past? Historical mutual aid societies also had limitations - often excluded women, minorities, disabled people.

**Scale constraints**: Can mutual aid work at larger scale? Or does it require small communities where markets or states might work better?

These aren't fully solved problems. The BFS experiments with mutual aid recognizing it's challenging, not claiming to have perfect answers.

### Why Mutual Aid Matters

Mutual aid is central to BFS because:

**Human interdependence**: We actually depend on each other. Mutual aid acknowledges and organizes this reality rather than denying it with individualist fantasy.

**Beyond market and state**: Market atomizes, state bureaucratizes. Mutual aid creates third alternative - voluntary cooperation among equals.

**Solidarity and community**: Building relationships of mutual commitment creates community. Not just living near each other but actually supporting each other.

**Empowerment**: Mutual aid means people have power to support each other, not depending on employers (market) or bureaucrats (state) for wellbeing.

**Human scale**: Mutual aid works at human scale - people helping people they know, based on shared understanding and commitment.

**[[Eudaimonia]]**: Human flourishing includes being part of communities of mutual support. Both giving and receiving help are part of living well.

The BFS wagers that recovering mutual aid as central organizing principle can create more humane and resilient communities than relying primarily on markets or state provision. Whether this works at the scale and in the context of modern life remains to be seen - but it's rooted in practices that worked for centuries and continue to emerge wherever people face hardship together.
