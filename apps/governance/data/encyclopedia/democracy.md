# Democracy

## What It Is

Democracy, from the Greek *dēmokratia* (δημοκρατία) - literally "rule by the people" - was a system of government in which political power resided with the citizenry rather than a monarch, aristocracy, or oligarchy.

The core principle was that citizens themselves made collective decisions about their community's affairs. This could take different forms: direct democracy, where citizens voted on laws and policies themselves, or representative democracy, where citizens elected officials to make decisions on their behalf.

Democracy was distinguished by several key features: equality before the law (*isonomia*), freedom of speech (*isegoria*), and the accountability of officials to the citizenry. The democratic ideal held that legitimate government derived its authority from the consent of the governed.

## History

**Ancient Athens (508-322 BCE):**

The world's first democracy emerged in Athens under the reforms of Cleisthenes. Key institutions included:
- The *ekklesia* (Assembly): All male citizens could attend, speak, and vote on laws and policy
- The *boule* (Council of 500): Selected by lot to prepare business for the Assembly
- The *dikasteria* (People's Courts): Large citizen juries decided legal cases
- Most offices filled by [[Sortition]] (random selection) rather than election

Athenian democracy was direct and participatory. Citizens didn't elect representatives; they were the legislature. Important decisions required debate and vote by the assembled citizenry.

**Medieval and Renaissance experiments:**

After Athens, democratic governance largely disappeared for two millennia. Some medieval city-states (Italian communes, Swiss cantons) developed oligarchic republics with limited citizen participation, but these were not democracies in the Athenian sense - power remained concentrated among wealthy families.

**Modern representative democracy:**

The American and French revolutions (late 18th century) inaugurated the modern era of representative democracy:
- Citizens elected representatives to legislative bodies
- Representatives made laws; citizens did not vote directly on legislation
- Initially limited to property-owning white men; gradually expanded through suffrage movements
- The United States Constitution (1787) created a republic with democratic elements but numerous anti-majoritarian features (Electoral College, Senate, lifetime judicial appointments)

**Contemporary challenges:**

By the early 21st century, democratic systems faced significant strain:
- **Oligarchic capture**: Wealth concentration allowed small groups to dominate political processes through campaign finance, lobbying, and media control
- **Voter disengagement**: Declining participation rates, particularly among younger and poorer citizens
- **Institutional gridlock**: Partisan polarization made compromise and governance increasingly difficult
- **Information warfare**: Propaganda, disinformation, and algorithmic manipulation undermined informed deliberation
- **Plutocracy**: Studies suggested that policy outcomes reflected elite preferences rather than median voter preferences

Some political scientists questioned whether "representative democracy" had become effective oligarchy with democratic aesthetics - elections that ratified elite power rather than checked it.

## In the Benjamin Franklin Society

The BFS implements a deliberative democracy that draws more from Athenian direct democracy than modern representative systems.

### Direct Participation

Members don't elect representatives to make decisions for them. Instead, citizens participate directly in governance through:
- **Referenda**: Direct votes on constitutional amendments and major policy questions
- **Petitions**: Citizen-initiated proposals that, with sufficient support, advance to referendum
- **Assembly participation**: Opportunities to address the General Assembly and participate in deliberation

### Sortition-Based Deliberative Bodies

Rather than elections, the BFS uses [[Sortition]] to constitute deliberative bodies like the General Assembly. Randomly selected citizens serve temporary terms, deliberate on society business, and make decisions within their delegated authority.

This combines elements of Athenian democracy (citizen participation, sortition) with modern innovations (structured deliberation, written procedures, documentation).

### Rationale

The BFS model addresses failures observed in contemporary representative democracy:

**Against oligarchy**: Random selection prevents wealth from determining who governs. All citizens have equal probability of selection regardless of resources.

**Real participation**: Citizens vote directly on foundational questions rather than delegating all decisions to representatives. Governance is not a spectator sport.

**Informed deliberation**: Sortition bodies receive time, information, and structured process to deliberate thoroughly before deciding - unlike voters choosing among candidates based on advertising.

**Accountability through rotation**: Short terms and inability to seek reselection mean decision-makers return to live under the rules they create. They govern their peers, not subjects.

**Civic equality**: Every member can speak, propose, and vote. Political power is not professionalized or concentrated in an elected class.

The BFS understands democracy not as a system of periodic elections, but as the practice of citizens governing themselves collectively through deliberation and direct decision-making.
