# Republic

## What It Is

A republic (from Latin *res publica*, "public thing" or "public affair") is a form of government where political authority is understood to belong to the public and is exercised for the common good, rather than being the private possession of a monarch or ruling class.

The essence of republicanism is not merely the absence of monarchy, but the positive commitment that government exists to serve the public interest, not private advantage. In a genuine republic:
- Authority belongs to the citizenry collectively, not to rulers personally
- Officials are accountable to the public and serve limited terms
- Citizens actively participate in governance
- Law governs, not the arbitrary will of rulers
- The common good takes precedence over factional or private interests

The term is often confused with "representative democracy" in modern usage, but historically referred to something more specific: a regime where citizens exercised collective self-governance, officials rotated regularly and remained accountable, and civic [[Virtue]] sustained public life.

A republic was contrasted with:
- **Monarchy**: Rule by a single person whose authority was personal/hereditary
- **Tyranny**: Rule by one person for their own benefit
- **Oligarchy**: Rule by a wealthy few for their own benefit
- **Mob rule** (*ochlocracy*): Unrestrained majority pursuing immediate desires without regard for justice or common good

The republic was the regime where free citizens governed themselves collectively for their common benefit.

## History

**Roman Republic (509-27 BCE):**

The Roman Republic emerged after overthrowing the last king and established institutions designed to prevent tyranny:

**Consuls**: Two chief executives, elected annually, who could veto each other. Power was shared and temporary, not concentrated in one person permanently.

**Senate**: Assembly of former magistrates providing continuity and counsel. Not democratic (composed of elite families) but accountable to public opinion and constrained by other institutions.

**Tribunes of the Plebs**: Officials elected by common citizens with power to veto Senate actions and protect plebeians from patrician abuse. Ensured that elite power faced popular check.

**Mixed constitution**: Combined elements of monarchy (consuls), aristocracy (Senate), and democracy (popular assemblies). Each element checked the others, preventing any from dominating.

**Civic virtue**: Romans emphasized virtues essential to republican life - *virtus* (courage, excellence), *pietas* (duty), devotion to the *res publica* over private interest. The ideal citizen sacrificed personal advantage for common good.

**Regular rotation**: Annual elections for most offices, strict term limits, and prohibition on consecutive terms for consulship ensured no one could dominate permanently.

**Rule of law**: Romans prided themselves on being governed by law, not the arbitrary will of rulers. Even the powerful were (theoretically) subject to law.

The Roman Republic inspired later republican thought, though in practice it remained oligarchic - power concentrated among elite families despite formal checks.

**Renaissance city-states:**

Medieval and Renaissance Italian city-states revived republican government:

**Florence, Venice, Genoa** and others developed republican institutions in the 13th-16th centuries:
- Elected councils and magistrates rather than hereditary rule
- Complex systems of rotation, [[Sortition]], and term limits to prevent domination
- Citizen militias rather than mercenary armies
- Strong civic culture and participation
- Suspicion of concentrated power

**Machiavelli** (*Discourses on Livy*, 1517) articulated republican political theory:
- Republics require civic virtue among citizens
- Freedom depends on active participation and willingness to defend the republic
- Mixed constitution balances popular and elite elements
- Conflict between social classes can be productive if properly channeled
- Republics are more stable and powerful than principalities because citizens fight for their own freedom

However, Renaissance republics often excluded large portions of residents from citizenship, limiting participation to propertied males of certain families.

**American founding debates:**

The American Revolution (1776) created a new republic, sparking intense debates about what republican government required:

**Anti-Federalists** emphasized:
- Small-scale republics where citizens could know each other and participate directly
- Rotation in office and term limits
- Citizen militias rather than standing armies
- Suspicion of concentrated power, especially distant national government
- Direct accountability of officials to local communities

**Federalists** argued:
- Large republic could work through representation
- Extended sphere would prevent any single faction from dominating
- Strong national government necessary for stability and security
- Separation of powers and checks and balances would prevent tyranny

The Constitution (1787) created a compound republic - neither pure democracy nor traditional aristocracy, but a representative system with federal structure, separation of powers, and limited suffrage.

**Key tension**: Was representation a practical necessity (republics must be small for direct participation, so large nations require representatives)? Or was representation actually superior to direct participation (elected elites would be wiser than common people)?

The Federalists increasingly embraced the latter view - representation as filter to refine popular opinion through elite deliberation. This moved away from classical republican emphasis on broad citizen participation.

**19th-20th century transformations:**

The term "republic" became increasingly ambiguous:

**Democratic republics**: Expanded suffrage movements gradually extended voting rights, but republican emphases on civic virtue, rotation, citizen participation, and public good over private interest faded.

**People's republics**: Communist states claimed to be republics serving the people, though they were typically single-party dictatorships. "Republic" became nearly meaningless as label.

**"Republic vs. democracy" rhetoric**: In the United States, some used "We're a republic, not a democracy" to defend anti-majoritarian institutions (Electoral College, Senate) or to oppose democratic reforms. This confused republic (government for public good) with oligarchy (rule by few).

**Modern electoral systems**: By the late 20th century, most "republics" meant merely:
- No hereditary monarch
- Some form of elections
- Constitutional framework

Lost were the substantive republican commitments: active citizen participation, rotation of office, civic virtue, public good over private interest, hostility to concentrated wealth and power.

**Contemporary collapse:**

By the early 21st century, nominal republics faced crises that revealed their hollowness:

**United States**: Studies suggested government policy reflected preferences of economic elites and business interests, not median voters. Wealth concentration, career politicians serving for decades, corporate lobbying, revolving door between government and industry, regulatory capture - all antithetical to republican principles.

**Voter disengagement**: Low participation, voter suppression, gerrymandering, and general sense that voting didn't matter reflected collapse of active citizenship.

**Factionalism**: Partisan tribalism replaced civic virtue and concern for common good. Politics became team sport, not collective deliberation about public interest.

**Privatization**: Public goods increasingly privatized, commons enclosed, government services outsourced to contractors. The "public thing" was being liquidated.

The form of republicanism (elections, constitutions) persisted while the substance (citizen self-governance for common good) disappeared. What remained were oligarchies with republican aesthetics.

## In the Benjamin Franklin Society

The BFS attempts to recover substantive republicanism - government of, by, and for the public, oriented toward common good rather than private advantage.

### The Public Thing

BFS takes seriously the meaning of *res publica* - the public thing, public affair:

**Collective ownership**: The society's resources, institutions, and governance belong to the membership collectively. No private owners extract profit from common institutions.

**Public deliberation**: Decisions are made through public processes - assemblies, referenda, open deliberation. Not in private negotiations among elites, not by unaccountable officials.

**Common good orientation**: Institutions exist to serve member flourishing ([[Eudaimonia]]), not to enrich particular individuals or factions. Success is measured by how well members collectively live, not by private accumulation.

**Transparency**: Governance processes are open to member observation and participation. Officials act in public view, accountable to citizenry.

### Active Citizenship

Republican government requires active citizens, not passive consumers of government services:

**Participation**: Through [[Sortition]], [[Referendum]], [[Petition]], and assembly deliberation, members actively participate in self-governance. Citizenship is not mere legal status but active practice.

**Civic duty**: Members have obligations to the republic - to participate in governance when selected, to deliberate on referenda, to serve the common good. Rights come with responsibilities.

**Civic virtue**: The republic depends on [[Virtue]] - practical wisdom, justice, courage, temperance, concern for common good over private advantage. Institutions are designed to cultivate these virtues.

**Political equality**: All members have equal standing as citizens. No separate political class, no hierarchy of political rights based on wealth or status.

### Rotation and Accountability

Following Roman and Renaissance precedents, BFS prevents concentration of power through rotation:

**[[Sortition]]**: Random selection for the General Assembly and other bodies ensures rotation. No one can build a political career or dominate governance permanently.

**Term limits**: Service is temporary. Members selected for offices serve their term and return to ordinary citizenship, living under rules they helped create.

**No professional politicians**: Governance is civic duty, not career. Members don't compete for offices or cultivate political brands. Selection is by lot, service is temporary, return to ordinary life is automatic.

**Accountability**: Short terms mean those exercising authority remain accountable. They cannot build insulated power bases or become distant from membership.

### Mixed Constitution

Like classical republics, BFS combines different elements:

**Democratic element**: Direct votes through [[Referendum]], open assemblies, equal participation through [[Petition]]. The people decide fundamental questions directly.

**Deliberative element**: [[Sortition]]-selected bodies (General Assembly, committees) deliberate on complex issues requiring time and information. Not direct democracy on everything, but representative deliberation through randomly-selected citizens.

**Constitutional element**: Fundamental law constrains both deliberative bodies and popular majorities. Not pure democracy where 50%+1 can do anything, but constitutional framework requiring supermajorities and referendum for changes.

This mixture aims to capture benefits of each:
- Direct democracy ensures popular [[Sovereignty]]
- Deliberative bodies enable careful consideration of complex questions
- Constitutional constraints prevent tyranny of temporary majorities

### Against Faction

Republican theory always worried about faction - groups pursuing private interest at expense of common good:

**Economic factionalism**: [[Oikonomia]] economics reduces incentives for wealth accumulation that could fund factionalism. Without great disparities of wealth, harder for economic faction to dominate.

**Structural barriers**: [[Sortition]] prevents formation of political parties or factions competing for offices. No campaigns, no party machinery, no factional competition for power.

**Common good discourse**: Deliberation is structured around arguments about common good, not factional advantage. Proposals must be justified in terms of benefit to the whole, not private interest.

**Solidarity**: Practices of mutual aid and collective provision cultivate sense that members share common fate. "An injury to one is an injury to all" expresses this republican solidarity.

### Citizen Militia Principle

Classical republics insisted on citizen militias rather than professional armies, believing:
- Citizens defending their own freedom fight better than mercenaries
- Standing armies become instruments of tyranny
- Military service is part of civic duty
- Professional warriors develop interests separate from the republic

BFS applies this principle beyond military affairs:

**All governance is citizen governance**: No professional administrators or permanent bureaucracy. Citizens temporarily selected perform governance functions and return to ordinary life.

**All work serves the republic**: Economic activity provisions the membership, not enriching separate ownership class. Members work for collective benefit.

**Security is collective**: Mutual aid and collective provision mean members secure each other, not depending on external authorities or market providers.

The principle: everything essential to republican life should be performed by citizens for each other, not outsourced to professionals with separate interests.

### Public vs. Private

Republicans drew sharp distinctions between public and private:

**Public goods**: Collectively provided - infrastructure, education, healthcare, security. Not commodified for private profit.

**Private life**: Family, friendship, personal pursuits protected from collective intrusion. Republic enables private flourishing but doesn't dictate it.

**Public service**: Temporary assumption of public duties for common good. Not permanent career or source of private enrichment.

**Private advantage vs. common good**: When conflicts arise, common good takes precedence. Cannot enrich yourself at collective expense.

The republic maintains both - robust public sphere of collective self-governance, and protected private sphere of personal life.

### Republican Liberty

Republican freedom meant something specific: not being subject to arbitrary power, living under laws you collectively make, having equal standing with fellow citizens.

This differs from modern "liberty" understood as absence of constraint:

**Republican liberty**: Living as free citizen among equals, participating in collective self-governance, not dominated by others. Freedom is political condition achieved collectively.

**Modern liberty**: Individual autonomy, freedom from interference. Freedom is individual possession, maximized by removing constraints.

BFS freedom is republican: members are free because they collectively govern themselves, because no one dominates them, because they have equal political standing. Not because they have no obligations or constraints, but because obligations are self-imposed through collective decision.

### Limits and Challenges

BFS recognizes challenges facing republican government:

**Scale**: Classical republics were small. BFS attempts to maintain republican character while potentially growing larger through careful institutional design.

**Civic virtue**: Republics require virtuous citizens. BFS creates conditions for [[Virtue]] cultivation but cannot guarantee all members will develop it.

**External pressures**: Operating within larger systems (capitalism, state power) that are anti-republican creates constant pressure. Maintaining republican character requires vigilance.

**Not utopian**: Republic is form of government, not paradise. Conflicts arise, mistakes are made, injustices occur. The question is whether they can be addressed through republican processes.

### Rationale

Restoring substantive republicanism addresses contemporary political failures:

**Against oligarchy**: Republican rotation, equality, and civic virtue prevent concentration of power in wealthy elite.

**Against professional political class**: Sortition and term limits prevent formation of career politicians separated from ordinary citizens.

**Against privatization**: Public goods remain truly public, collectively governed for common benefit.

**Against factionalism**: Structural barriers and common good discourse reduce factional competition.

**For self-governance**: Citizens actually govern themselves collectively, not just elect distant representatives.

**For accountability**: Short terms and rotation ensure those exercising authority remain accountable to their peers.

**For common good**: Institutions orient toward collective flourishing, not private enrichment.

The BFS is a republic in the substantive sense: the public affair, collectively governed by citizens for the common good. Not merely a system without a king, but a positive form of collective self-governance by free and equal citizens.
