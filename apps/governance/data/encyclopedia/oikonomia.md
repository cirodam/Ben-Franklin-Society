# Oikonomia

## What It Is

Oikonomia (οἰκονομία) is the ancient Greek term for household management - the art of acquiring and using resources to sustain and flourish a household or community. The word combines *oikos* (household) and *nomos* (law/management), literally meaning "household law" or "household order."

In classical philosophy, oikonomia referred to the natural and proper acquisition of wealth: getting what you need to live well. It was fundamentally about provisioning - ensuring the household had food, shelter, clothing, tools, and other necessities for a good life.

This was contrasted with **chrematistike** (χρηματιστική) - the art of wealth acquisition for its own sake, unlimited money-making disconnected from actual needs or use. While oikonomia was considered natural and necessary, chrematistike was viewed as unnatural and corrupting.

The English word "economy" derives from oikonomia, but has largely lost the original meaning. Modern "economics" studies markets, prices, and growth, not household provisioning or the art of living well with resources.

## History

**Aristotle's philosophy:**

Aristotle (*Politics*, Book I, circa 350 BCE) made crucial distinctions about wealth acquisition:

**Natural wealth acquisition (oikonomia proper):**
- Aimed at acquiring what the household needs for the good life
- Had natural limits - you need only so much food, so many tools, so much shelter
- Included farming, herding, hunting, fishing - direct production of useful things
- Also included natural exchange - trading surplus goods for things you need
- Was part of living virtuously and achieving *eudaimonia* (human flourishing)

**Unnatural wealth acquisition (chrematistike):**
- Aimed at unlimited accumulation of money for its own sake
- Had no natural limits - you could always want more money
- Focused on exchange itself as a source of profit, not on use-value
- Included retail trade pursued purely for profit and especially usury (lending money at interest)
- Distorted the purpose of wealth from provision to accumulation

Aristotle's critique of usury was particularly sharp. Money, he argued, was meant to facilitate exchange of useful goods. Using money to breed more money (charging interest) perverted its natural function. The Greek word for interest, *tokos*, literally means "offspring" - Aristotle found it absurd and unnatural that barren metal should "give birth."

**Key principle**: Wealth exists to serve life; accumulation beyond need becomes an end in itself that corrupts character and community.

**Medieval Christian economics:**

Medieval Christian thinkers, influenced by Aristotle, developed similar distinctions:

**Thomas Aquinas** (13th century) argued:
- Buying and selling for necessary sustenance was legitimate
- Seeking profit as an end in itself was sinful avarice
- Charging interest (usury) violated natural law and divine command
- The "just price" should reflect costs and labor, not maximize profit

The Catholic Church formally prohibited usury, though enforcement varied and exceptions multiplied over time. The prohibition reflected understanding that lending at interest could trap borrowers in perpetual debt and extract wealth without productive labor.

**Sumptuary laws** in medieval and early modern Europe attempted to limit luxury consumption, reflecting the view that wealth beyond need was morally suspect and socially destabilizing.

**Displacement by capitalism:**

The rise of commercial capitalism (16th-18th centuries) gradually displaced oikonomia with chrematistike as the organizing principle of economic life:

- Protestant Reformation (particularly Calvinism) reinterpreted wealth accumulation as evidence of divine favor rather than sin
- Banking and finance expanded despite usury prohibitions, with increasing theological justifications
- Colonialism and slave trade generated enormous profits divorced from productive household economy
- Mercantilism prioritized national wealth accumulation (gold reserves) over household provisioning
- Early factories separated workers from household production

**Modern economics:**

By the 19th-20th centuries, oikonomia had been entirely displaced:

- **Adam Smith** (*Wealth of Nations*, 1776) founded modern economics on the principle that individual pursuit of profit (chrematistike) would benefit society through the "invisible hand"
- Classical and neoclassical economics treated unlimited wants as natural and economic growth as the primary goal
- GDP and economic growth became measures of success, regardless of whether growth served actual human needs
- Debt-based finance became normal - mortgages, credit cards, student loans
- Usury prohibitions disappeared; interest became simply "the price of money"
- Corporations existed solely to maximize shareholder returns, with no concept of sufficiency

The modern economy operated on chrematistike principles:
- Unlimited accumulation was rational and desirable
- Money should breed more money through investment returns
- Growth was imperative; steady-state economy was stagnation
- Profit was the measure of economic activity, not provision for need

**Contemporary consequences:**

By the early 21st century, chrematistike economics produced:
- Enormous wealth concentration: billionaires while millions lacked basic needs
- Financialization: profits from moving money around exceeded profits from making useful things
- Household debt crisis: mortgages, student loans, credit card debt as normal conditions
- Ecological destruction: unlimited growth impossible on finite planet
- Meaningless work: jobs that generated profit without serving genuine needs
- Chronic anxiety: never enough money, always needing more

The very concept that economic activity should provision household needs for flourishing had been lost. Economy was no longer oikonomia - it was pure chrematistike.

## In the Benjamin Franklin Society

The BFS attempts to restore oikonomia as the foundation of economic life - organizing resources for household and community provisioning rather than unlimited accumulation.

### Community Banking

BFS community banks embody oikonomia principles versus chrematistike finance:

**Oikonomia banking:**
- Purpose is provisioning: providing members access to resources they need for good lives
- Credit for productive purposes: tools, housing, education, business equipment
- Interest (if charged) covers administrative costs, not profit maximization
- Money serves exchange of real goods and services, not financial speculation
- Success measured by member flourishing, not bank profit

**Versus chrematistike banking:**
- Purpose is profit extraction: maximizing returns for shareholders
- Credit as commodity: sold to whoever will pay the highest interest
- Interest rates set to maximize bank profit, regardless of borrower circumstances
- Money breeds money through derivatives, speculation, arbitrage
- Success measured by quarterly earnings and stock price

### Labor Compensation

BFS labor compensation reflects oikonomia understanding of wealth as provision for need:

- Compensation aims to provision household needs, not to accumulate unlimited wealth
- Based on actual costs of living well - food, shelter, healthcare, education, leisure
- Not based on "market rates" which can be decoupled from need
- Recognizes that beyond provisioning sufficiency, additional monetary compensation doesn't increase flourishing
- Surplus value goes to collective benefit rather than individual accumulation

This rejects the chrematistike assumption that rational individuals always want more money regardless of whether they need it.

### Economic Purpose

The BFS economy exists to provision member households, not to maximize growth or returns:

**Oikonomia goals:**
- Every household has sufficient food, secure housing, healthcare, education
- Work is meaningful and connected to genuine needs
- Resources are stewarded sustainably for long-term provisioning
- Economic surplus supports collective goods (infrastructure, culture, mutual aid)
- Success is measured by member flourishing, not monetary growth

**Not chrematistike goals:**
- GDP growth
- Shareholder returns
- Competitive accumulation
- Market expansion
- Profit maximization

### Limits and Sufficiency

Oikonomia recognizes natural limits to need. Unlike chrematistike, which treats wants as unlimited, oikonomia asks: "How much is enough?"

A household needs:
- Nutritious food (but not unlimited restaurant meals)
- Secure housing (but not unlimited square footage)
- Healthcare (but not unlimited cosmetic procedures)
- Education (but not unlimited credentialing)
- Tools and equipment for work and life
- Leisure time and cultural participation
- Security against misfortune

These needs can be met. Once met, additional wealth accumulation serves vanity, anxiety, or power - not genuine flourishing.

BFS organizes to provision these needs reliably, rather than to enable unlimited accumulation that inevitably means some have far beyond sufficiency while others lack basics.

### Usury and Interest

The BFS approach to credit reflects Aristotle's critique of usury. While some credit mechanisms may include cost recovery, the society rejects:
- Lending purely to extract profit from borrowers
- Interest rates that trap borrowers in perpetual debt cycles
- Using money to breed money without productive purpose
- Financial speculation and derivatives divorced from real production

Credit serves provisioning - helping members access resources before they could otherwise afford them - not profit extraction.

### Production for Use

Economic activity in BFS is oriented toward producing things people actually need and use, not toward generating profit regardless of social value:

- Agriculture produces food people eat, not commodity crops for financial speculation
- Housing construction creates homes people live in, not investment properties
- Healthcare serves patient wellbeing, not insurance company profits
- Education serves learning, not credentialing for labor market competition

This returns to the oikonomia principle that economic activity should serve the good life, not accumulation for its own sake.

### Rationale

Restoring oikonomia addresses fundamental problems of chrematistike economics:

**Against unlimited accumulation**: Once households are provisioned, additional accumulation serves no genuine human purpose. The drive for more becomes pathological rather than rational.

**Against debt servitude**: Chrematistike economics normalizes permanent debt. Oikonomia economics aims to provision households without trapping them in interest obligations.

**For ecological sustainability**: Oikonomia's recognition of sufficiency aligns with ecological limits. Unlimited growth is impossible; provisioning within limits is achievable.

**For meaningful work**: When economic activity aims at provisioning needs rather than profit, work can be organized around genuine human purposes rather than arbitrary jobs.

**For community stability**: Chrematistike economics requires constant competition and disruption. Oikonomia economics can be stable and predictable because needs are relatively stable.

**For human flourishing**: The good life requires reliable provision of needs, not unlimited wealth. Organizing economy for provisioning serves *eudaimonia* better than organizing for accumulation.

The BFS does not reject markets, trade, or monetary exchange. It rejects the principle that economic activity should pursue unlimited accumulation regardless of whether it serves human flourishing. It returns to the ancient understanding: the economy exists to provision households so people can live well together.
