# Virtue

## What It Is

Virtue (Greek: *arete*, ἀρετή; Latin: *virtus*) is excellence of character - a stable disposition to think, feel, and act well. A virtue is a trait that enables a person to flourish, to live well, to achieve [[Eudaimonia]].

The Greek *arete* meant excellence or goodness of any kind. A knife's *arete* is cutting well; a horse's *arete* is running swiftly. Human *arete* is living excellently as a human being - exercising our distinctive capacities (reason, sociability, choice) in excellent ways.

Virtues are not rules or principles to follow, but qualities of character developed through practice and habituation. A virtuous person doesn't merely act correctly; they have become the kind of person who naturally perceives, desires, and acts rightly. The courageous person isn't someone who follows rules about courage; they are someone who has cultivated the disposition to face danger appropriately.

Virtue was traditionally understood to include both:

**Moral virtues** (virtues of character):
- **Courage**: Facing fear and danger appropriately
- **Temperance**: Proper enjoyment of pleasures, moderation
- **Justice**: Giving each person their due, fairness
- **Generosity**: Appropriate giving and receiving
- **Wisdom/Prudence**: Practical judgment about how to live

**Intellectual virtues** (virtues of mind):
- **Theoretical wisdom**: Understanding of fundamental truths
- **Practical wisdom** (phronesis): Sound judgment about how to act in particular situations
- **Understanding**: Grasping first principles
- **Craft knowledge**: Excellence in making or producing

Virtue ethics held that living virtuously was both the means to and the substance of the good life. You don't become virtuous in order to be happy as a separate reward; living virtuously is itself living well.

## History

**Aristotle's virtue ethics:**

Aristotle (*Nicomachean Ethics*, circa 350 BCE) developed the most influential account:

**Virtue as mean between extremes:**
- Each virtue is a mean (middle point) between two vices - one of excess, one of deficiency
- Courage is the mean between cowardice (deficiency of confidence) and rashness (excess of confidence)
- Temperance is the mean between self-indulgence (excess) and insensibility (deficiency)
- Generosity is the mean between stinginess (deficiency) and wastefulness (excess)
- The mean is relative to the person and situation, requiring practical wisdom to discern

**Habituation and practice:**
- Virtues are not innate or taught theoretically - they're developed through practice
- We become just by doing just acts, courageous by doing courageous acts
- Moral education involves training in right habits from childhood
- Eventually, virtuous action becomes "second nature" - natural and pleasant

**The role of practical wisdom:**
- Moral virtues require phronesis (practical wisdom) to apply correctly
- Rules cannot capture what virtue requires in every situation
- The virtuous person has developed judgment to perceive what the situation calls for
- Practical wisdom is itself a virtue, developed through experience

**The unity of virtues:**
- True virtue requires having all virtues together
- Cannot be truly courageous without also being wise (knowing what's worth risking danger for)
- Cannot be truly generous without justice (giving to right people in right amounts)
- Practical wisdom connects the virtues

**Key principle**: Virtue is a disposition acquired through practice, guided by practical wisdom, aiming at the mean relative to circumstances.

**Roman virtue:**

Romans emphasized civic and martial virtues:
- *Virtus* originally meant manly courage and excellence in warfare
- Expanded to include civic virtues: justice, duty to republic, dignity, integrity
- *Gravitas* (seriousness, dignity), *pietas* (duty, respect), *dignitas* (worthiness) were Roman ideals
- Virtue was demonstrated through service to the republic and excellent public conduct

**Medieval Christian virtues:**

Medieval Christianity integrated classical virtues with theological commitments:

**The Cardinal Virtues** (from classical philosophy):
1. **Prudence** (practical wisdom)
2. **Justice** (rendering to each their due)
3. **Fortitude** (courage, endurance)
4. **Temperance** (moderation, self-control)

**The Theological Virtues** (from Christian revelation):
1. **Faith** (belief in God and divine truth)
2. **Hope** (trust in divine promises)
3. **Charity/Love** (love of God and neighbor)

Thomas Aquinas (13th century) synthesized Aristotelian and Christian accounts:
- Natural virtues could be developed through human effort and habituation
- But supernatural virtues (theological) required divine grace
- All virtues ultimately oriented toward union with God
- Christian humility contrasted with classical pride/honor

**Enlightenment and modernity:**

Modern philosophy largely abandoned virtue ethics:

**Social contract theory** (Hobbes, Locke): Focused on rights, rules, and institutions rather than character. The question became "What rules should govern us?" not "What kind of people should we become?"

**Utilitarianism** (Bentham, Mill): Judged actions by consequences (utility produced), not by agent's character. Rules replaced virtues as guides to action.

**Kantian ethics**: Emphasized duty and universal moral law, not character traits. The good person follows the categorical imperative, not cultivated dispositions.

**Liberal individualism**: Rejected shared conceptions of excellence or virtue as imposing values on individuals. Each person decides their own conception of good life.

Virtue ethics was dismissed as:
- Pre-modern and unscientific
- Culturally relative (virtues differ across societies)
- Potentially authoritarian (who decides what virtues are?)
- Vague compared to clear rules and principles

**Contemporary psychology:**

20th-21st century psychology partially revived interest in character:

**Character education movement**: Attempted to teach virtues in schools, though often reduced to rule-following rather than habituation.

**Positive psychology** (Seligman, Peterson): Identified "character strengths" - wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence - associated with wellbeing. However, treated them as individual traits, not social practices.

**Situationist critique**: Psychologists argued that behavior depends more on situations than stable character traits. People act virtuously in some contexts but not others, suggesting character doesn't exist as classical virtue ethics claimed.

**Modern failures:**

By the early 21st century, virtue language had become confused:

- "Virtue signaling" - performing moral righteousness for social status rather than genuinely embodying virtues
- Self-help culture focused on individual optimization and "life hacks" rather than character development through community
- Educational systems focused on job preparation and credentialing rather than cultivating excellent persons
- Social media rewarded performative outrage and tribal loyalty rather than practical wisdom and temperance
- Consumer culture encouraged self-indulgence rather than temperance
- Career competition encouraged strategic calculation rather than justice and generosity

The very concepts needed to discuss character formation had atrophied. Modern culture could produce skilled workers, enthusiastic consumers, and engaged citizens of spectacle, but lacked frameworks for cultivating virtue.

## In the Benjamin Franklin Society

The BFS recognizes that [[Eudaimonia]] (human flourishing) requires virtue, and virtue requires institutions and practices that cultivate it. Character is not innate or individually bootstrapped - it develops through participation in communities oriented toward excellence.

### Habituation Through Practice

Following Aristotle, BFS understands that virtue develops through practice, not instruction:

**Civic participation**: Members develop practical wisdom (phronesis) and civic virtue by actually participating in [[Sortition]] bodies, [[Referendum]]s, and [[Petition]]s. You learn to deliberate well by deliberating, to judge wisely by making judgments.

**Meaningful work**: Members develop excellence in their domains through practice in [[College]]s. The farmer becomes excellent at farming by farming, the teacher at teaching by teaching - not merely by studying theory.

**Community life**: Social virtues develop through actual community participation - generosity through occasions to give, friendship through shared activity, justice through having to navigate conflicts fairly.

### Practical Wisdom (Phronesis)

BFS institutions aim to develop practical wisdom - the ability to judge well in particular situations:

**Deliberation**: Governance structures require deliberation about complex questions with no clear right answers. This develops judgment rather than rule-following.

**Experience over theory**: Members learn by doing - serving in assemblies, working in colleges, participating in community decisions. Abstract principles are insufficient; wisdom requires experience.

**Mentorship**: Experienced practitioners mentor newcomers in colleges and governance roles, modeling excellent judgment and helping others develop it.

**Mistakes as learning**: The society recognizes that developing wisdom requires making mistakes and learning from them. Perfection isn't expected; growth is.

### The Mean and Moderation

BFS economics and culture reflect the principle of virtue as mean between extremes:

**Economic mean**: [[Oikonomia]] economics aims at sufficiency - the mean between poverty (deficiency) and luxury (excess). Provisioning adequate resources without encouraging unlimited accumulation.

**Work-leisure balance**: Neither overwork (excess) nor idleness (deficiency), but appropriate balance between productive contribution and restorative leisure.

**Political participation**: Neither apathy (deficiency) nor obsessive activism (excess), but appropriate engagement in civic life.

**Confidence**: Neither servility (deficiency) nor arrogance (excess), but appropriate self-respect and respect for others as co-equal citizens.

### Social Context of Virtue

Virtue is not individual achievement but social development:

**Community practices**: Virtues are cultivated through participation in shared practices - governance, work, ritual, celebration, mutual aid. The community provides contexts for virtue to develop.

**Role models**: Experienced members model excellent character for others. Virtue is partially learned by observing and emulating those who embody it.

**Social support**: The community supports members in developing virtue - encouraging growth, providing correction when someone acts badly, offering opportunities to practice excellent behavior.

**Recognition**: The society recognizes and honors virtuous behavior, reinforcing development of character. Not through competitive awards, but through genuine appreciation of excellence.

### Specific Virtues Cultivated

**Practical wisdom**: Developed through deliberation, sortition service, and experience making communal decisions. The capacity to judge well about how to live together.

**Justice**: Cultivated through fair distribution of resources ([[Oikonomia]]), equal political standing ([[Democracy]], [[Sortition]]), and practices of resolving conflicts fairly.

**Courage**: Not primarily martial courage but civic courage - willingness to speak honestly in assembly, to dissent from majority when necessary, to take on difficult tasks for community good.

**Temperance**: Cultivated through economic practices that provide sufficiency without encouraging unlimited consumption, and cultural practices that value moderation over excess.

**Generosity**: Developed through practices of contribution to common good, mutual aid, teaching and mentoring, sharing knowledge and resources.

**Friendship** (philia): Deep mutual regard developed through working together, deliberating together, celebrating together - the thick social bonds of genuine community.

**Solidarity**: Recognition of common membership and mutual obligation. "An injury to one is an injury to all" expresses this virtue.

**Craftsmanship**: Excellence in one's domain of work. Taking pride in doing things well, not merely efficiently or profitably.

### Colleges as Virtue Communities

BFS [[College]]s are communities oriented toward excellence in specific domains:

- Members pursue excellence together in agriculture, healthcare, education, arts, etc.
- More experienced practitioners mentor newcomers in both technical skill and professional virtue
- Standards of excellence are maintained collectively
- Knowledge is advanced through collective inquiry
- Virtue in domain is recognized and celebrated

Colleges provide contexts where domain-specific virtues can be cultivated - the particular excellences relevant to farming, healing, teaching, building, etc.

### Limits of Virtue Development

BFS recognizes virtue cultivation has limits:

**Time and effort**: Developing virtue requires years of practice and habituation. The society supports this development but cannot force it or rush it.

**Individual variation**: People differ in aptitudes and in willingness to engage in practices that cultivate virtue. The society creates opportunities but cannot guarantee outcomes.

**External constraints**: Operating within larger systems (capitalism, state power) creates pressures against virtue. Making prudent judgments is harder when external forces push toward vice (greed, fear, selfishness).

**Vice is possible**: The society cannot prevent all vice. Members may act badly despite good institutions. The goal is to tilt conditions toward virtue development, not to create perfect people.

### Against Modern Character Flaws

BFS virtue cultivation addresses problems of contemporary culture:

**Against individualism**: Virtue develops socially, not through isolated self-improvement. Character forms through participation in communities of practice.

**Against consumerism**: Temperance and sufficiency replace insatiable consumption. The good life is virtuous activity, not accumulation.

**Against careerism**: Excellence in domain replaces strategic positioning for advancement. Do good work, not impressive work.

**Against tribalism**: Practical wisdom and justice replace partisan loyalty and identity performance. Judge situations on merits, not tribal affiliation.

**Against nihilism**: Virtue provides objective standards of excellence. There are better and worse ways to live, not just individual preference.

**Against optimization**: Virtue is balance and wisdom, not maximization. The mean between extremes, not grinding everything to the limit.

### Virtue and Eudaimonia

Ultimately, virtue cultivation serves [[Eudaimonia]] - human flourishing:

- Virtuous activity is itself flourishing, not merely means to happiness
- The courageous, just, temperate, wise person is living well
- Virtue enables us to live together excellently in community
- Character development is personal growth toward full actualization of human potential

BFS institutions exist to enable virtue development because virtue is both constitutive of and necessary for the good life. Creating conditions where people can become excellent versions of themselves - practically wise, just, courageous, temperate, generous - is creating conditions for human flourishing.

The society doesn't claim to make people virtuous, but it does claim to provide better conditions for virtue cultivation than prevailing alternatives. Communities of practice, meaningful work, civic participation, sufficiency without excess, mentorship and habituation - these are contexts where character can develop.

The question is not whether institutions shape character (they always do), but whether they shape it toward virtue or vice. BFS attempts the former.
