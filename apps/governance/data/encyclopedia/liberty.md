# Liberty

## What It Is

Liberty, in political philosophy, was the condition of being free - not subject to arbitrary power or domination by others. But what "being free" meant has been deeply contested throughout history.

Classical and republican traditions understood liberty primarily as **non-domination**: not being subject to the arbitrary will of another. A free person was not at anyone's mercy, not dependent on another's whim or favor. Liberty meant having secure standing, not vulnerable to another's arbitrary interference.

This differed from later liberal conceptions of liberty as **non-interference**: merely being left alone. Someone could be left alone (not currently interfered with) while still being subject to another's power - at their mercy, dependent on their forbearance. Republican liberty required security against domination itself, not just absence of current interference.

And both differed from **positive liberty** or **autonomy**: self-mastery, governing yourself according to reason rather than passion, or collective self-governance. This conception emphasized freedom as achievement requiring certain capacities and conditions.

**Key distinctions:**

**Liberty vs. License**: Liberty was not unlimited freedom to do whatever you pleased regardless of effects on others. License - unrestrained action without regard to right or others - was different from liberty. True liberty existed within law and community, not outside them.

**Freedom from domination vs. freedom from interference**: Being free meant not being subject to another's arbitrary power, not merely being left alone. A slave whose master happened not to interfere was still unfree - at the master's mercy.

**Individual vs. collective**: Was liberty individual independence or collective self-governance? Republicans emphasized self-governance - free people governed themselves collectively. Modern liberals often emphasized individual independence - doing what you want without interference.

**Economic liberty**: Did liberty require economic independence, or was it compatible with wage labor and market dependence? Could you be politically free while economically dependent?

These different conceptions had radically different implications for how to organize political and economic life.

## History

**Roman *libertas*:**

Roman concept of liberty contrasted free citizen with slave:

**Slave vs. free**: Slave was *in potestate* - subject to master's arbitrary power. Master could do anything to slave at whim. Free person had legal standing, rights, protections - not subject to arbitrary power.

**Citizens as free**: Roman citizens had *libertas* - protected by law, participating in governance, not subject to arbitrary will of kings or masters.

**Mixed constitution**: Romans believed liberty required mixed constitution balancing different elements - consuls, senate, tribunes, assemblies. Prevented any one from dominating.

**Virtuous citizens**: Liberty required virtuous citizens willing to defend it. Corruption and luxury led to tyranny.

**Economic base**: Liberty traditionally required economic independence - property ownership, not dependence on others. Client-patron relationships were acceptable if regulated, but complete dependence meant unfreedom.

This conception: liberty as non-domination, requiring legal protections, political participation, mixed constitution, citizen [[Virtue]].

**Republican tradition:**

Renaissance Italian republics revived Roman conception:

**Florentine liberty**: Machiavelli argued free republics required citizen militia, mixed government, virtue, laws that no one person controlled. Liberty meant collective self-governance, not domination by prince or faction.

**Venetian model**: Mixed constitution balancing doge, senate, council. Checks preventing domination by any part.

**Against monarchy**: Republicans argued monarchy was incompatible with liberty. Even "good king" left subjects at his mercy - dependent on his virtue rather than secured by law and institutions.

English republicans (Sidney, Harrington, Milton) made similar arguments against Stuart absolutism. Liberty required constitutional government, balance, rule of law, citizens armed and participating.

**Liberal revolution:**

17th-19th centuries saw shift toward liberal conceptions:

**Hobbes**: Liberty as "absence of external impediments." Free to do what you have power to do, unless physically prevented or legally prohibited. No talk of domination or dependence.

**Locke**: Natural liberty to do what you judge best for self-preservation, within bounds of natural law. Civil liberty as freedom under rule of law protecting property and person. More individualist than republican - liberty as independence, not collective self-governance.

**Classical liberalism**: By 19th century, dominant view was negative liberty - government shouldn't interfere with individuals' choices, especially economic choices. Free markets as realm of liberty.

**Berlin's "Two Concepts"**: Isaiah Berlin (1958) distinguished negative liberty (absence of interference) from positive liberty (self-mastery, autonomy). Worried positive liberty could become totalitarian - forcing people to be free according to some vision of their "real" will.

Liberal tradition emphasized: individual rights, limited government, rule of law, private property, free markets, toleration, pluralism. Liberty increasingly meant being left alone to pursue your own conception of good life.

**Socialist critique:**

19th-20th century socialists challenged liberal liberty:

**Formal vs. real freedom**: Legal right to do something isn't freedom if you lack means. "Freedom to dine at Ritz" meaningless if you can't afford it. Liberty requires material conditions.

**Wage slavery**: Wage laborers weren't truly free - dependent on employers for livelihood, subject to employers' power during work, precarious if fired. Market dependence was form of domination.

**Positive liberty**: Real freedom required collective control over production, planning for human needs, ending exploitation. Self-governance in economic as well as political sphere.

**Marx**: "Realm of freedom" began where labor determined by necessity ended. Capitalism kept people unfree through need to work for others. Communism would enable genuine freedom through abundance and collective ownership.

Socialists argued liberal liberty was hollow - formal legal rights without material means, political democracy without economic democracy, freedom for capitalists but domination for workers.

**20th century developments:**

**Totalitarianism**: Nazi and Soviet regimes claimed to represent "true" freedom while crushing all dissent. Led to deep suspicion of positive liberty and collective projects.

**Cold War polarization**: West emphasized individual liberty, free markets, limited government. East emphasized economic security, collective welfare, planning. Each accused other of tyranny.

**Civil rights and liberation movements**: Expanded understanding of domination to include racial oppression, patriarchy, colonialism. Liberty required ending these forms of domination, not just formal equality.

**Neoliberalism**: Late 20th century saw revival of classical liberalism - liberty primarily as free markets, property rights, limited government, individual choice. Freedom conflated with consumer choice.

**Republican revival**: Scholars (Skinner, Pettit, others) revived republican concept of liberty as non-domination. Argued it differed from both negative liberty (non-interference) and positive liberty (autonomy).

By early 21st century, multiple incompatible conceptions of liberty competed: libertarian (minimal state, maximum markets), liberal (rights and tolerance), socialist (economic democracy), republican (non-domination), communitarian (self-governance in community).

## In the Benjamin Franklin Society

The BFS recovers republican conception of liberty as non-domination and collective self-governance.

### Liberty as Non-Domination

Core conception: freedom means not being subject to arbitrary power of others.

**Domination defined**: Someone dominates you when they have power to interfere arbitrarily in your choices - when you're at their mercy, dependent on their will, vulnerable to their whim.

**Not just non-interference**: Master who happens not to interfere with slave still dominates slave. Benevolent employer who happens to treat worker well but could fire at whim still exercises domination. Liberty requires security against domination, not just good fortune that powerful choose not to interfere.

**Arbitrary power**: Power is arbitrary when it can be exercised at the powerful person's discretion, according to their judgment or whim, without having to answer to those affected. Even if power is exercised benevolently, if it's at discretion of powerful rather than controlled by law or affected parties, it's domination.

**Examples of domination:**
- Absolute monarch over subjects (even if benevolent)
- Master over slave (even if kind)
- Employer over at-will employee (even if decent)
- Husband over wife in patriarchal marriage (even if loving)
- Landlord over tenant (even if reasonable)
- Any relationship where one party is at mercy of another's uncontrolled discretion

Liberty requires ending these relationships of domination, not merely hoping powerful exercise power benevolently.

### Self-Governance

Liberty means governing ourselves collectively:

**Not being ruled**: Free people govern themselves; they aren't ruled by others - kings, oligarchs, bureaucrats, experts, corporations.

**Collective self-governance**: [[Democracy]] in BFS means citizens collectively making decisions affecting their shared life. Not having decisions made for them by others.

**Participation**: Liberty requires actually participating in governance, not just having representatives who claim to act for you. [[Sortition]] ensures widespread participation through rotation.

**Small scale essential**: Can only genuinely govern yourself in community small enough to actually participate meaningfully. In mass societies, "self-governance" becomes fiction - really ruled by bureaucracies, parties, corporate power.

**Deliberation**: [[Deliberation]] means reasoning together about what to do, not being told what to do or manipulated into supporting decisions made elsewhere.

This is positive conception - liberty as achievement, requiring certain structures and practices, not just absence of interference.

### Economic Liberty

Liberty requires economic independence from domination:

**Wage labor as domination**: Traditional employment relationship involves domination - employer has discretionary power over employee. Can fire, assign tasks, set conditions. Employee dependent on employer's decisions.

**Market dependence**: Needing to sell labor to survive means being vulnerable to those who control jobs. Even "free labor market" involves domination when one party needs work to live, other can choose whether to employ.

**[[Mutual Aid]] economy**: BFS provisioning system means members aren't dependent on employers for necessities. Healthcare, food, housing, childcare provided through reciprocal support system. Not needing wage labor for survival removes major source of domination.

**Community Bank**: Access to credit without commercial bank power. No loan officer exercising discretionary power over your livelihood.

**Commons**: Shared resources mean no one controls what you need. Can't be dominated through control of essential resources.

**Services and colleges**: Contributing through service rather than wage labor means not subject to employer power. Working with others, not for others.

This doesn't eliminate all markets or exchange, but removes domination from economic necessities. Economic base for liberty.

### Preventing Political Domination

BFS structures prevent concentration of political power:

**[[Sortition]]**: Random selection prevents electoral politicians from gaining power over others. Can't build political machines or followings that enable domination.

**Rotation**: Brief terms and mandatory rotation prevent anyone accumulating lasting power. No permanent political class.

**Distributed authority**: No single powerful office where power concentrates. Authority distributed across Assembly, offices, courts, colleges.

**Accountability**: Those exercising authority answer to equals, not ruling over subjects. Peer accountability prevents arbitrary power.

**Rule of law**: Even Assembly bound by charter and laws. Can't exercise unlimited power at discretion.

**Small scale**: Community small enough that members know each other, can check power personally, not dominated by distant bureaucracy.

These structures create what republicans called "mixed constitution" - balancing different elements so none can dominate.

### Against Domination, Not Against Authority

Liberty as non-domination doesn't mean no one ever directs others:

**Non-arbitrary authority**: Authority that must answer to those affected, that follows law, that can be challenged isn't domination. Teachers direct students, but within rules, answerable to community, students can appeal.

**Temporary and rotational**: Brief service in authority followed by return to equality prevents domination. Not permanent hierarchy.

**Functional and bounded**: Authority in specific domain for specific purpose isn't domination of whole person. Healthcare provider has authority regarding medical treatment, not your whole life.

**Contestable**: Can challenge authoritative decisions through deliberation, appeals, referendums. Not unquestionable power.

**Reciprocal**: Take turns exercising authority through rotation. You direct others, they direct you at different times. Reciprocity prevents domination.

The goal isn't eliminating all authority but ensuring authority isn't arbitrary, permanent, unaccountable, or comprehensive. Legitimate authority differs from domination.

### Liberty and Law

Republican conception: liberty exists within law, not outside it:

**Law protects liberty**: Law prevents domination by limiting what people can do to each other. "Rule of law not men" means no one has arbitrary power.

**Self-imposed law**: But law must be self-imposed - made by those who live under it through collective deliberation. Laws imposed by rulers would be domination.

**Against license**: Liberty is not doing whatever you want regardless of effects on others. Laws preventing domination - prohibiting slavery, assault, fraud, domination - don't reduce liberty but constitute it.

**Within bounds**: Living under law you helped make, that prevents domination and serves common good, is freedom. Not constraint on liberty but condition for it.

This challenges both anarchist rejection of law and libertarian view that all law constrains freedom. Republican view: right laws constitute liberty by preventing domination.

### Liberty Requires Community

Can't be free alone or through pure independence:

**Collective power**: Resisting domination requires collective power. Isolated individuals are vulnerable. Only together can people resist powerful actors.

**Solidarity**: [[Mutual Aid]] and [[Solidarity]] create conditions for liberty. Mutual support means not dependent on any one employer, landlord, or powerful individual.

**Shared governance**: Self-governance is inherently collective. Can't govern just yourself - need to participate in collective decisions about shared life.

**Republican virtue**: Maintaining liberty requires citizens who participate, who watch for domination, who defend republican institutions. [[Virtue]] sustains liberty.

**Against individualism**: Modern individualist fantasy - being completely independent, self-sufficient, free from dependence on others - is illusion that leaves people vulnerable to domination. Real liberty requires interdependence within free community.

Paradox: liberty requires binding yourself to community through mutual commitments. But these bonds freely entered and reciprocal differ from domination.

### What Liberty Doesn't Mean

BFS rejects certain conceptions of liberty:

**Not consumer choice**: Market libertarians conflate liberty with consumer choice. But being free to choose products isn't political liberty, especially when underlying economic relationships involve domination.

**Not being left alone**: Pure negative liberty - being left alone to do what you want - isn't the goal. That's compatible with vulnerability to domination, with being at others' mercy. Security against domination matters more than just current non-interference.

**Not unlimited markets**: "Free markets" often involve domination - employer over employee, creditor over debtor, property owner over those who need resources. Market freedom for some means domination of others.

**Not statelessness**: Anarchist ideal of no authority often means vulnerable to domination by anyone stronger. Law and institutions, properly structured, protect liberty rather than constrain it.

**Not private property absolutism**: Absolute property rights enable domination - landlord over tenant, owner over worker. Commons and limits on property can enhance liberty by preventing domination.

**Not doing whatever you want**: Liberty doesn't mean freedom to dominate others. Your liberty ends where you would exercise arbitrary power over others.

These negative clarifications matter: avoiding confusion with libertarianism, anarchism, or apolitical "personal freedom."

### Trade-offs and Tensions

Liberty involves real tensions:

**Liberty and equality**: Does liberty require rough equality (so no one has power to dominate)? Or does equalizing involve constraining liberty? BFS view: gross inequality enables domination, so rough equality required for liberty.

**Liberty and community**: Do community expectations constrain liberty? Or does community enable liberty by providing base for collective self-governance? BFS: community obligations freely entered are compatible with liberty; domination by individuals isn't.

**Liberty and security**: Does securing liberty require constraints on action? Laws preventing domination constrain what people can do. BFS: constraints on domination enhance liberty rather than reduce it.

**Individual and collective**: Is liberty individual independence or collective self-governance? BFS: both - but individual independence requires collective power to prevent domination.

**Scale limits**: Liberty as collective self-governance works at small scale. At mass scale, becomes representative government where most don't actually participate - not true self-governance. BFS accepts this means societies must stay small.

These tensions don't have clean solutions but must be navigated through [[Practical Wisdom]] and deliberation.

### Why Liberty Matters

Liberty is central to [[Eudaimonia]]:

**Human dignity**: Beings capable of reason and judgment shouldn't be subject to arbitrary power of others. Dignity requires security against domination.

**Self-determination**: Humans flourish when governing themselves rather than being ruled. Self-governance is intrinsic good, not just instrumental.

**Development of capacities**: Being free person - participating in governance, exercising judgment, taking responsibility - develops human capacities. Domination stunts development.

**Intrinsic value**: Liberty is component of human flourishing itself, not merely means to other goods. Free life is better than unfree life, even if materially equivalent.

**Against degradation**: Domination degrades both dominated and dominator. Master becomes corrupted by arbitrary power; slave becomes servile. Liberty enables both to be fully human.

For BFS, liberty isn't just political goal but part of what it means to live well as human being.

### The Wager

BFS wagers that small-scale republican liberty works better than alternatives:

**Against libertarianism**: Pure market freedom produces domination - employer over worker, wealthy over poor, property owner over those who need resources. "Freedom" for some to dominate isn't liberty.

**Against statism**: Massive state bureaucracies govern *for* people, not *by* them. Even benevolent bureaucracy is domination - people subject to administrative discretion, not governing themselves.

**Against individualism**: Fantasy of pure independence leaves people vulnerable. Real security against domination requires community.

**Small-scale self-governance**: Communities small enough for genuine participation, with economic base (mutual aid) preventing domination, with institutions distributing power and enabling rotation - this can achieve real liberty.

Challenges:
- Does small scale mean most people excluded from any particular society?
- Can mutual aid economy really prevent all economic domination?
- Will informal hierarchies and domination emerge despite formal structures?
- Is this romanticizing small communities that historically often had oppressive norms?

These are real questions. But BFS wagers this approach better realizes liberty than mass electoral democracy with market economies (domination by employers, politicians, bureaucrats) or authoritarian collectivism (domination by party, state).

The test: can small communities of equals governing themselves collectively, with economic base preventing domination, achieve real liberty? Or will new forms of domination emerge? The society is experiment in whether republican liberty can work in modern context.
