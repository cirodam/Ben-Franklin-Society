# College

## What It Is

Traditionally, a college was a community of scholars and practitioners organized around a shared field of knowledge or practice. The term derives from the Latin *collegium*, meaning "community" or "society" - a group of colleagues bound together by common purpose.

In contemporary American usage, "college" has become largely synonymous with undergraduate higher education - a degree-granting institution offering bachelor's degrees. The original meaning as a community of learning, where knowledge was preserved, transmitted, and advanced through collective effort, has largely been lost in favor of a more bureaucratic and credentialing function.

## History

The concept of the college emerged in medieval Europe with the rise of universities.

**Medieval colleges:**
- Originated in 12th-13th century universities (Paris, Bologna, Oxford)
- Initially residential communities where students and masters lived together
- Provided housing, meals, libraries, and instruction
- Famous examples: Oxford and Cambridge colleges, each a self-governing community within the larger university
- Colleges at Oxford like Balliol (1263) and Merton (1264) still exist today

**The collegiate model:**
- Combined living and learning in a single community
- Masters (teachers) and scholars (students) ate, studied, and debated together
- Each college had its own governance, endowment, and traditions
- Fostered intensive mentorship and intellectual discourse
- The tutorial system: small-group or one-on-one instruction

**American adaptation:**
- Colonial colleges (Harvard 1636, William & Mary 1693, Yale 1701) adapted the model
- "Liberal arts colleges" emerged emphasizing broad education over specialized training
- The college became associated with undergraduate education specifically
- Land-grant colleges (Morrill Act 1862) added practical agricultural and mechanical arts

**Other collegiate traditions:**
- College of Cardinals (Catholic Church): body of senior clergy who elect the Pope
- Electoral College (US): assembly that formally elects the President
- Royal Colleges (Britain): professional bodies for physicians, surgeons, etc.

## In the Benjamin Franklin Society

BFS colleges organize members around the society's various domains of knowledge and practice, combining the educational mission of traditional colleges with practical application.

### Structure

Each major area of societal activity has an associated college:
- **Agriculture College**: Agricultural science, sustainable farming, food systems
- **Energy College**: Power generation, conservation, energy systems
- **Healthcare College**: Medical knowledge, healing practices, public health
- **Education College**: Pedagogy, curriculum, knowledge transmission
- **Arts College**: Creative practices, cultural preservation, aesthetic knowledge
- And so forth for each domain

### Functions

**Knowledge preservation**: Colleges maintain libraries, archives, and repositories of accumulated knowledge in their field. The Agriculture College might preserve heirloom seed collections and farming techniques; the Arts College might maintain records of local traditions and cultural practices.

**Education and mentorship**: Colleges coordinate the training of new members entering their field. This includes formal instruction, apprenticeships with experienced practitioners, and continuing education for all members. The tutorial model persists: experienced practitioners mentor newcomers in small groups or individually.

**Advancement of knowledge**: Colleges are communities of inquiry. Members collectively study their field, experiment with new approaches, document their findings, and advance understanding. Research and practice inform each other.

**Standards and practice**: While primarily educational, colleges also help maintain quality standards in their domain. The Healthcare College might establish care protocols; the Education College might develop curriculum guidelines.

**Work coordination**: College members coordinate to meet the society's needs in their domain, applying collective knowledge to practical problems.

**Governance**: Colleges are largely self-governing, establishing their own procedures and selecting leadership (often by [[Sortition]]). They operate within the bounds of the society's charter but manage their own internal affairs.

### Membership

Members join colleges based on their interests, skills, and areas of study or practice. Membership is about learning and contribution to a field of knowledge, not merely practicing a trade. A member might belong to multiple colleges if they study multiple domains.

The college is both an educational institution and a community of practice - a place to learn, to teach, to study, and to apply knowledge in service to the society.
