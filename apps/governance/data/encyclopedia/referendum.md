# Referendum

## What It Is

A referendum (plural: referenda or referendums) is a direct vote by the electorate on a specific political question, law, or policy proposal. Rather than electing representatives to decide an issue, citizens vote directly on the matter itself.

The term comes from the Latin *referendum*, meaning "that which must be referred" - something referred to the people for their decision. Referenda allow voters to approve or reject laws, constitutional amendments, policy changes, or other governmental decisions.

Referenda can be binding (the result becomes law) or advisory (the result guides officials but doesn't have legal force). They can be initiated by government officials, legislative bodies, or citizens through petition processes.

## History

**Ancient origins:**

The Roman Republic (509-27 BCE) featured popular assemblies where citizens voted directly on laws and declarations of war. These *comitia* were early forms of direct democracy, though they operated under significant aristocratic influence.

**Swiss development:**

Modern referenda emerged in Switzerland beginning in the 13th century with local assemblies (*Landsgemeinden*) where citizens gathered to vote directly on cantonal matters. The Swiss Confederation developed the most extensive referendum system in the world:

- The 1848 Swiss Constitution established mandatory referenda for constitutional amendments
- The 1874 Constitution added optional referenda, allowing citizens to challenge laws passed by parliament
- Popular initiatives (1891) enabled citizens to propose constitutional amendments by petition
- Switzerland continues to hold federal referenda several times per year on diverse topics

**American usage:**

The United States adopted referenda primarily at the state and local levels:

- South Dakota (1898) was the first state to establish the initiative and referendum
- By 1918, 19 states had adopted initiative and referendum processes
- California became famous (or notorious) for extensive use of ballot propositions
- The federal government has no provision for national referenda

Criticism emerged that ballot measures could be dominated by special interests with resources to gather signatures and fund campaigns, sometimes producing contradictory or poorly drafted laws.

**Other democratic nations:**

Many democracies used referenda for major constitutional questions:
- Brexit referendum (UK, 2016): Whether to leave the European Union
- Irish referenda on abortion (2018) and same-sex marriage (2015)
- Constitutional referenda in France, Australia, Canada, and elsewhere

However, national referenda remained relatively rare in most representative democracies, typically reserved for fundamental constitutional changes rather than ordinary legislation.

**Critiques:**

Critics argued that referenda could:
- Reduce complex issues to yes/no questions
- Enable majority tyranny over minority rights
- Be manipulated through misleading campaign rhetoric
- Produce inconsistent results when voters approve contradictory measures
- Advantage wealthy interests who could fund signature-gathering and advertising

Defenders countered that referenda provided a necessary check on unresponsive or captured legislatures.

## In the Benjamin Franklin Society

The BFS uses referenda as the primary mechanism for direct [[Democracy]], allowing members to vote directly on laws and major policy questions.

### Constitutional Referenda

All amendments to the society's charter require referendum approval. No representative body can alter the fundamental constitution without direct consent of the membership. This ensures that foundational rules can only change through broad consensus.

### Legislative Referenda

Major legislative questions can be referred to the membership for direct vote. The General Assembly may choose to refer consequential decisions to the full membership rather than deciding itself.

### Citizen-Initiated Petitions

Members can initiate referenda through the petition process:
1. A member drafts a proposal (constitutional amendment, law, policy change)
2. The proposal is published for member review
3. Other members may sign the petition in support
4. Upon reaching the required threshold of signatures, the proposal advances to referendum
5. All members vote on the question
6. If approved by the required majority, the proposal becomes law

This citizen-initiative process ensures that governance is not monopolized by sortition bodies. Any member can propose changes, and if enough fellow citizens agree the question is important, it goes to a direct vote.

### Deliberation Before Voting

Unlike typical referendum campaigns dominated by advertising, BFS referenda are preceded by structured deliberation:
- Proposals are published with sufficient time for review
- The General Assembly or proposing member may provide explanatory materials
- Members can discuss proposals in forums and assemblies
- Questions and concerns can be raised and addressed

The goal is informed voting based on deliberation rather than reaction to marketing campaigns.

### Rationale

Referenda serve several purposes in BFS governance:

**Popular sovereignty**: Members retain ultimate decision-making authority on fundamental questions. Representatives cannot alter the constitution or enact major changes without direct consent.

**Check on representatives**: Even sortition bodies, randomly selected, could make decisions the broader membership opposes. Referenda provide a corrective mechanism.

**Citizen agency**: The petition process enables any member to propose changes. Governance is not limited to those selected for office; any citizen can initiate action.

**Democratic legitimacy**: Laws approved by direct vote carry stronger legitimacy than representative decisions. When members collectively decide, they cannot later claim laws were imposed upon them.

**Civic engagement**: The referendum process encourages members to understand issues, deliberate with peers, and actively participate in self-governance.

The BFS views referenda not as occasional exceptions to representative rule, but as routine exercises of collective self-governance - the citizenry directly deciding their own affairs.
